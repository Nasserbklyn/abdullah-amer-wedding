# Herbal/botanical supplements for erectile function, libido and male sexual performance (daily use): evidence and safety

Scope note: Covers Panax ginseng (Korean red ginseng, KRG), maca, Tribulus terrestris, Tongkat ali (Eurycoma longifolia), fenugreek (incl. Testofen), ashwagandha, horny goat weed/icariin, yohimbe/yohimbine, saffron, ginkgo, Butea superba, and combination products. Human RCTs and meta-analyses are prioritised; animal/in-vitro data are labelled "preclinical". Network constraint during research: NCBI/PubMed/Cochrane hosts were not reachable directly from the sandbox; abstracts were retrieved via the Firecrawl proxy and the Europe PMC API, so a few items (noted in Gaps) could only be partly verified.

## Key question 1: Which botanicals have the strongest human evidence for erectile function (vs libido or testosterone), and how large are the effects?

### Takeaway
Only Panax ginseng and Tribulus terrestris have multiple placebo-controlled RCTs with validated erectile-function (IIEF) endpoints, and in both cases the pooled benefit is small (ginseng: IIEF-EF +3.5 points, IIEF-5 +2.4 points, below the minimal clinically important difference; Tribulus: IIEF-5 +3.2 points driven mainly by one industry-linked trial). Maca, saffron, Tongkat ali, fenugreek and ashwagandha have either single small trials or outcomes that are mainly libido/testosterone rather than erectile function; icariin has no human ED trials; yohimbine has older trial support but a poor safety/regulatory profile.

### Cited Findings
- Ginseng (Cochrane 2021, 9 RCTs, 587 men with mild-moderate ED, follow-up <=12 weeks): IIEF-15 erectile-function domain mean difference (MD) 3.52 points (95% CI 1.79-5.25; I²=0%; 3 studies; low certainty) against an assumed MCID of 4; IIEF-5 MD 2.39 (95% CI 0.89-3.88; I²=0%; 3 studies; moderate certainty) against an MCID of 5; self-reported ability to have intercourse RR 2.55 (95% CI 1.76-3.69; 6 studies; low certainty), i.e. 321 more men per 1000 (95% CI 158-558 more) vs 207/1000 on placebo; adverse events RR 1.45 (95% CI 0.69-3.03; 7 studies; low certainty) — [Lee et al., Cochrane Database Syst Rev 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33871063/)
- Ginseng, earlier pooled estimates: Borrelli 2018 meta-analysis (24 RCTs, 2,080 patients overall; five ginseng monopreparation RCTs, n=399) found ginseng improved IIEF-5 with SMD 0.43 (95% CI 0.15-0.70; P<0.01; I²=0; 140 ginseng vs 96 placebo) — [Borrelli et al., Drugs 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29633089/); Jang 2008 systematic review (7 RCTs, low average quality; 6 placebo-controlled, n=349) gave a response risk ratio of 2.40 (95% CI 1.65-3.51; I²=22%) and 2.05 (95% CI 1.33-3.16) in psychogenic ED — [Jang et al., Br J Clin Pharmacol 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18754850/)
- Key ginseng RCTs: Hong 2002 crossover (45 men, KRG 900 mg three times daily, 8 weeks each arm): mean IIEF 38.1 ± 16.6 on KRG vs 30.9 ± 15.7 on placebo (baseline 28.0; p<0.01), 60% reported improved erection on the global efficacy question, RigiScan tip rigidity improved — [Hong et al., J Urol 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12394711/); Choi 2013 multicentre RCT (119 men, mild-moderate ED, standardized Korean ginseng berry 4 x 350 mg/day = 1,400 mg/day, 8 weeks): IIEF-15 total rose from 40.95 ± 7.05 to 46.19 ± 12.69 and the EF domain only from 17.17 ± 2.57 to 18.59 ± 5.99 (p<0.05 within-group), hormone and lipid markers unchanged — [Choi et al., Int J Impot Res 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23254461/)
- Tribulus terrestris: Kamenov 2017 phase IV, multicentre, double-blind RCT (180 men aged 18-65 with mild-moderate ED with/without HSDD; Tribestan® [Sopharma AD, Bulgaria] 250 mg film-coated tablets of 35-45:1 dry extract standardized to >=112.5 mg furostanol saponins, 3 x 2 tablets/day = 1,500 mg extract/day, 12 weeks): ITT IIEF difference vs placebo 2.70 points (95% CI 1.40-4.01; P<0.0001); intercourse satisfaction (p=0.0005), orgasmic function (p=0.0325), sexual desire (p=0.0038), overall satisfaction (p=0.0028) and GEQ (p<0.0001) also favoured Tribulus; no difference in adverse events — [Kamenov et al., Maturitas 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28364864/); an independent commentary raised conflict-of-interest concerns about the product's sponsorship — [Relias Media commentary](https://reliasmedia.com/articles/141517-tribulus-an-rct-supporting-its-use-for-erectile-dysfunction)
- Tribulus, negative trial: Santos 2014 (30 men, 800 mg/day, 30 days) concluded Tribulus was not more effective than placebo for ED symptoms or serum total testosterone — [Santos Jr et al., Actas Urol Esp 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24630840/) (details per [Elsevier abstract page](https://www.elsevier.es/en-revista-actas-urologicas-espanolas-english-392-articulo-tribulus-terrestris-versus-placebo-in-S2173578614000523))
- Tribulus, pooled: Suharyani 2025/2026 meta-analysis (8 studies): vs placebo IIEF-5 MD 3.23 (95% CI 1.89-4.58; p<0.00001) and IIEF-15 MD 14.44 (95% CI 5.75-23.14; p=0.001); no significant difference in total testosterone; no difference in adverse events — [Suharyani et al., Int J Impot Res 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40360723/); Borrelli 2018 (2 Tribulus RCTs, n=202) classed results as "mixed" — [Borrelli 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29633089/)
- Maca: the only ED-specific RCT (Zenico 2009; 50 men with mild ED; 2,400 mg/day maca dry extract; 12 weeks) found IIEF-5 improved +1.6 vs +0.5 points with placebo (P<0.001) — a small difference — [Zenico et al., Andrologia 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19260845/) (figures per [full-text PDF](https://cdn.shopify.com/s/files/1/1788/9345/files/Andrologia_-_2009_-_Zenico_-_Subjective_effects_of_Lepidium_meyenii_Maca_extract_on_well_being_and_sexual_performances_in.pdf)); Gonzales 2002 (healthy men 21-56 y, 1,500 or 3,000 mg/day gelatinized maca, 12 weeks) showed improved self-reported sexual desire from week 8, independent of Hamilton depression/anxiety scores and of testosterone/oestradiol, which did not change — [Gonzales et al., Andrologia 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12472620/)
- Saffron: the largest trial (346 treatment-naive men; open-label crossover, on-demand sildenafil vs saffron 30 mg twice daily, 12 weeks each) found no improvement in any IIEF domain, SEP or EDITS with saffron; GEQ "yes" 91.2% sildenafil vs 4.2% saffron (P=0.0001) — [Safarinejad et al., Int J Impot Res 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20520621/); in contrast, in 36 men with fluoxetine-related sexual impairment, saffron 15 mg twice daily for 4 weeks improved IIEF erectile-function (P<0.001) and intercourse-satisfaction (P=0.001) domains and total score, with 60% vs 7% reaching normal EF (>25) (P=0.005), but not orgasm, overall satisfaction or desire — [Modabbernia et al., Psychopharmacology 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22552758/)
- Tongkat ali, erectile function: Kotirum 2015 meta-analysis (2 RCTs, 139 participants): IIEF-5 change at week 12 WMD 0.91 (95% CI -1.50 to 3.33; I²=89.5%; not significant); subgroup with lower baseline IIEF-5 +2.15 (95% CI 1.03-3.27) — [Kotirum et al., Complement Ther Med 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26365449/)
- Ashwagandha, sexual function: Khanna 2026 RCT (76 healthy men 30-50 y, standardized root extract 300 mg twice daily, 8 weeks) reported improvements vs placebo in SDI-2, satisfying sexual events and overall IIEF (p<=0.001), plus +36% ejaculate volume, +38% sperm count, +87% motility; no adverse events — [Khanna et al., Front Reprod Health 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41766918/); Chauhan 2022 (50 men with low sexual desire, 300 mg twice daily, 8 weeks): DISF-M total improved vs placebo (mean difference 9.8; 95% CI 8.87-10.73) and serum testosterone increased — [Chauhan et al., Health Sci Rep 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35873404/); a critical appraisal of the KSM-66 trial noted no correction for multiple comparisons across secondary endpoints — [Natural Health Research Institute summary](https://naturalhealthresearch.org/ashwagandha-root-extract-ksm-66-and-male-sexual-health-evidence-from-an-8-week-randomized-controlled-trial/)
- Fenugreek: Steels 2011 (60 healthy men 25-52 y without ED; Testofen 600 mg/day, 6 weeks): DISF-SR arousal and orgasm sub-domains improved; prolactin and testosterone stayed within reference range — [Steels et al., Phytother Res 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21312304/); Rao 2016 (120 healthy men 43-70 y; Testofen 600 mg/day, 12 weeks): Aging Male Symptom score fell vs placebo, sexual function (morning erections, frequency of sexual activity) improved, total and free testosterone rose vs placebo — [Rao et al., Aging Male 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26791805/)
- Yohimbine: Ernst & Pittler 1998 meta-analysis of 7 placebo-controlled monotherapy trials: odds ratio 3.85 (95% CI 2.22-6.67) for superiority over placebo; "serious adverse reactions were infrequent and reversible" — [Ernst & Pittler, J Urol 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9649257/); criticisms include heterogeneous ED aetiology, subjective endpoints, lack of ITT and poor adverse-event reporting — [Relias Media review](https://reliasmedia.com/articles/42147-yohimbine-for-the-treatment-of-impotence)
- Butea superba: Cherdshewasart 2003 3-month randomized double-blind trial (Thai men 30-70 y, crude tuber preparation) reported significant improvement in 4 of 5 IIEF-5 items and "noticeable improvement" in 82.4%, with no haematology/chemistry changes — [Cherdshewasart & Nimsakul, Asian J Androl 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12937809/); an open-label comparison (32 men, 100 mg Butea vs sildenafil 50 mg) found 84% vs 81% responders in phase 1, but the effect vanished in the double-blind phase with a second batch, and the supplier later confirmed the first batch had been blended with a PDE5 inhibitor — [Cortés-González et al., BJU Int 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19624593/)
- Icariin / horny goat weed: no published human ED trial was found; evidence is in-vitro PDE5 inhibition (semi-synthetic icariin derivatives were ~80-fold more potent than icariin, approaching sildenafil) — PRECLINICAL — [Dell'Agli et al., J Nat Prod 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18778098/); and rat models (e.g., improved erectile function in spontaneously hypertensive rats via eNOS uncoupling) — PRECLINICAL — [PubMed 29968380](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29968380/); Mayo: "not enough evidence to show that it helps for ED" — [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/erectile-dysfunction/in-depth/erectile-dysfunction-herbs/art-20044394)
- Ginkgo: the two placebo-controlled trials in antidepressant-induced sexual dysfunction were negative — Kang 2002 (n=37; 19 ginkgo vs 18 placebo; 8 weeks): no difference at weeks 2, 4, 8; both groups improved — [Kang et al., Hum Psychopharmacol 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12404672/); Wheatley 2004 triple-blind (24 patients, ginkgo 240 mg/day, 12 weeks): "no statistically significant differences, and no differences in side-effects" — [Wheatley, Hum Psychopharmacol 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15378664/); the oft-cited 84% response came from an uncontrolled open trial (dose 60 mg once daily to 120 mg twice daily, mean 209 mg/day) — [Cohen & Bartlik, J Sex Marital Ther 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9611693/)
- Cross-botanical pooled view: Ho 2025 meta-analysis (14 RCTs, 1,227 men with ED, follow-up 1-6 months) found herbal supplements overall improved erectile function SMD 1.20 (95% CI 0.64-1.76), orgasmic function SMD 1.09, sexual desire SMD 1.02, intercourse satisfaction SMD 1.48, overall satisfaction SMD 0.93 and testosterone SMD 0.54 (95% CI 0.04-1.05); saffron and ginseng drove the EF benefit while evidence for Tribulus and maca was "insufficient" — [Ho et al., J Tradit Complement Med 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41696741/)
- Network meta-analysis of nutraceuticals (15 RCTs, 1,000 men): only L-arginine (SUCRA 52%) and propionyl-L-carnitine + acetyl-L-carnitine + sildenafil (97%) showed statistically significant efficacy; "no other treatment regimen showed efficacy with statistical significance" against "a background of general ineffectiveness of most nutraceutical interventions" — [Barbonetti et al., J Sex Med 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39279185/)

### Inferences
- Ranked by erectile-function evidence (quantity and validated endpoints): ginseng > Tribulus > saffron (conflicting) > maca ≈ Tongkat ali > ashwagandha (one small trial) > fenugreek (libido/T only) > Butea superba (adulteration confounds) > icariin/ginkgo (no supportive controlled human data).
- Even for the best-supported herbs, the pooled IIEF gains (2-3.5 points) are below the MCIDs used by Cochrane (4 for IIEF-EF, 5 for IIEF-5), so effects are best described as "trivial to small" compared with PDE5 inhibitors (sildenafil +87.6% IIEF-EF vs +9.8% in the saffron crossover).
- Libido and testosterone outcomes (fenugreek, ashwagandha, Tongkat ali, maca) are measured in healthy or low-desire men, not men with ED; they should not be presented as ED treatments.

### Gaps
- Could not retrieve the abstract/numbers of de Andrade 2007 (60 men, KRG 1,000 mg three times daily, 12 weeks) — [PubMed 16855773](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16855773/) returned a cookie wall.
- Sample size of Cherdshewasart 2003 is not stated in the abstract.
- The 2021 Turkish Journal of Urology yohimbine systematic review could not be opened (PMC cookie wall); only a search-engine summary was available.

## Key question 2: What did the Cochrane review (Lee 2021) and other systematic reviews (2018-2026) conclude?

### Takeaway
Cochrane 2021 concluded ginseng "may only have trivial effects on erectile function or satisfaction with intercourse" (mostly low-certainty evidence), while later reviews (2021-2026) agree that ginseng, Tribulus, Pycnogenol/L-arginine combinations and possibly saffron show small, preliminary signals, that most other botanicals lack adequate trials, and that no herbal product is supported as first-line therapy.

### Cited Findings
- Cochrane authors' conclusions verbatim: "Based on mostly low certainty evidence, ginseng may only have trivial effects on erectile function or satisfaction with intercourse compared to placebo when assessed using validated instruments. Ginseng may improve men's self-reported ability to have intercourse. It may have little to no effect on adverse events. We found no trial evidence comparing ginseng to other agents with a more established role in treating erectile dysfunction, such as phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors." No study reported quality of life; no predefined subgroup analyses were possible; search date 30 January 2021 — [Lee et al., Cochrane 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33871063/); summary version published in [World J Mens Health 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34169686/)
- Borrelli 2018 (Drugs): 24 RCTs/2,080 patients; "encouraging evidence suggests that ginseng may be an effective herbal treatment"; Pinus pinaster and maca "very preliminary"; saffron and Tribulus "mixed"; only 7 trials had predominantly low risk of bias; median Jadad score 3/5; adverse events recorded in 19/24 trials with no verum-placebo difference; "more research ... is justified before the use of herbal products in ED can be recommended" — [Borrelli et al. 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29633089/)
- Leisegang & Finelli 2021 (Arab J Urol; 42 placebo-controlled trials with IIEF as primary outcome): Panax ginseng, Pycnogenol/Prelox and Tribulus terrestris "have some promising evidence as herbal products, alongside L-arginine"; only 42 studies were included, "making any conclusions relatively unclear even for the more prominent interventions"; maca "may cause psychological adverse events, including anxiety, mood swings, hallucinations, and addictive behaviour" (citing Corazza 2014) — [PMC8451697](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8451697/)
- Petre 2023 (Nutrients; marketed-product analysis plus meta-analysis): Panax ginseng, Tribulus terrestris and L-arginine "are able to improve male sexual function", but "none of the studies carried out provided sufficient evidence to consider these products a first-line therapy"; of supplements marketed in Italy, 80% matched the "no expected efficacy" criterion and only 8% the higher-efficacy cluster; products "are usually blends of many substances that are frequently employed at a negligible dose or without any evidence" — [Petre et al., Nutrients 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37686709/)
- Barbonetti 2024 network meta-analysis: see Q1; concluded "general ineffectiveness of most nutraceutical interventions" except L-arginine and PLC+ALC with PDE5i in organic ED — [Barbonetti et al. 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39279185/)
- Ho 2025 (J Tradit Complement Med): 14 RCTs/1,227 men; saffron effective for EF, orgasmic function and intercourse satisfaction; ginseng for EF, orgasm, desire, overall satisfaction and testosterone; Tribulus and maca evidence "insufficient"; authors declared no competing interests — [Ho et al. 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41696741/)
- Suharyani 2026 (Tribulus-specific, 8 RCTs) concluded Tribulus "may offer benefits in improving erectile function ... with a relatively good safety profile" — [Suharyani et al. 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40360723/)
- Maca-specific reviews: Shin 2010 (4 RCTs) found "limited evidence" with too few trials/participants to draw firm conclusions — [Shin et al., BMC CAM 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20691074/); a 2023 meta-analysis identified only two eligible RCTs (one positive, one negative for erectile function) — [J Men's Health 2023 via DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/0bffc13f512145da88b1dd7cd736af43); a 2026 narrative review says most trials used ~3 g/day maca powder, findings in men are consistently positive for libido but "additional research is needed" — [Bloomer, 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42680584/)
- Tongkat ali reviews: testosterone meta-analysis (9 studies; 5 RCTs pooled) SMD 1.352 (95% CI 0.565-2.138; p=0.001) with 87% heterogeneity and significant publication bias (Egger/Begg) in the hypogonadism subgroup; authors declared no conflicts and no external funding — [Leisegang et al., Medicina 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9415500/); erectile function meta-analysis non-significant (Q1) — [Kotirum 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26365449/)
- Fenugreek reviews: Isenmann 2023 (7 studies, 449 participants, 378 male): total testosterone SMD 0.32 (95% CI 0.09-0.55), free testosterone SMD 0.24 (95% CI -0.04 to 0.52), lean mass SMD 0.19 (ns), leg press SMD 0.22 (ns) — [Isenmann et al., Int J Sports Med 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37253363/); Mansoori 2020 (4 trials) reported a significant total-testosterone effect — [Mansoori et al., Phytother Res 2020 (summary)](https://nutrition-evidence.com/article/345822/effect-of-fenugreek-extract-supplement-on-testosterone-levels-in-male-a-meta-analysis-of-clinical-trials); a 2026 Frontiers in Nutrition meta-analysis of placebo-controlled trials found SMD 0.25 (p=0.037) for total testosterone with imprecise/heterogeneous free-testosterone results — [Frontiers in Nutrition 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2026.1903508/full)
- Ashwagandha hormonal meta-analysis (23 RCTs, 1,706 participants; PROSPERO CRD42024611576): testosterone in men MD +57.43 ng/dL (not in women, MD +5.09), cortisol SMD -1.18, serotonin MD +31.75 ng/mL, T4 MD +0.61 µg/dL (p=0.02), no effect on TSH/T3 or oestradiol; "heterogeneity and limited data on some endpoints warrant further standardized trials" — [Fornalik et al., Planta Med 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41740946/)

### Inferences
- The field's reviews converge: ginseng is the only herb with consistent (if trivial-to-small) RCT support for erectile function; Tribulus is positive in pooled analyses but hinges on Kamenov 2017; saffron results depend on population (SSRI-induced vs organic ED) and design (open-label vs blinded).
- Positive-leaning meta-analyses (Ho 2025 SMD 1.20) pool heterogeneous small trials with large effect sizes that are implausible relative to Cochrane's trial-level MDs; the Cochrane/GRADE assessment is the more conservative anchor.

### Gaps
- Full texts of Ho 2025 and Suharyani 2026 (individual trial list, risk-of-bias detail) were not retrieved; only abstracts.

## Key question 3: Evidence quality per botanical (maca, Tribulus, Tongkat ali, fenugreek, ashwagandha, icariin, saffron, ginkgo, Butea)

### Takeaway
Across these botanicals the pattern is: few trials (1-8 per herb), small samples (20-180), short duration (4-12 weeks), frequent manufacturer involvement or proprietary extracts (Tribestan, Physta, Testofen, KSM-66), outcomes that are often libido/testosterone surrogates rather than erectile function, and at least one documented case (Butea superba) in which an apparently positive "herbal" result was explained by undisclosed PDE5-inhibitor adulteration.

### Cited Findings
- Maca: 4 RCTs in the 2010 systematic review (2 positive for desire/dysfunction in menopausal women and healthy men, 1 negative in healthy cyclists, 1 positive in ED by IIEF-5); "total number of trials, the total sample size, and the average methodological quality of the primary studies were too limited to draw firm conclusions" — [Shin 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20691074/); maca did not alter LH, FSH, prolactin, 17-OH-progesterone, testosterone or oestradiol at 1.5 or 3 g/day over 12 weeks — [Gonzales et al., J Endocrinol 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12525260/); SSRI-induced dysfunction pilot (20 remitted depressed outpatients, 17 women): 3.0 g/day improved ASEX (22.8 to 16.9; P=0.028) and MGH-SFQ, 1.5 g/day did not; only 10 completers — [Dording et al., CNS Neurosci Ther 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18801111/)
- Tribulus: trial-level conflict (Kamenov 2017 positive at 1,500 mg/day extract x 12 weeks vs Santos 2014 negative at 800 mg/day x 30 days) plus a placebo-controlled study in late-onset hypogonadism with ED/LUTS — [GamalEl Din et al., Urologia 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30253697/); no testosterone effect in the 2026 meta-analysis — [Suharyani 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40360723/); the positive pivotal trial used the manufacturer's branded product (Tribestan, Sopharma) — [Kamenov 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28364864/)
- Tongkat ali: essentially all trials used one proprietary water extract (Physta®, Biotropics Malaysia): Tambi 2012 (76 LOH men, 200 mg/day, 1 month; T 163 → 240 ng/dL, uncontrolled pre/post), Chinnappan 2021 (35 vs 35 men 50-70 y, 100 mg/day, 12 weeks; T 187 → 204 vs 183 → 178 ng/dL, p<0.05), Ismail 2012 (54 vs 55 men 30-55 y, 300 mg/day, 12 weeks; testosterone not significant), George 2013 (300 mg/day, 12 weeks; not significant), Chan 2021 (16 vs 16 healthy men 18-30 y, 600 mg/day, 2 weeks), Henkel 2014 (13 seniors, 400 mg/day, uncontrolled), Leitão 2021 (ADAM, 6-month RCT with exercise); only one study reported adverse effects (similar in both groups) — [Leisegang 2022 meta-analysis table](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9415500/); key trial records: [Tambi 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21671978/), [Chinnappan 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34262417/), [Ismail 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23243445/), [Leitão 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33541567/), Physta + Polygonum minus combination RCT [Udani 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24550993/); Examine's overall verdict: "some evidence exists for this effect in men with low testosterone and the mechanisms are plausible, [but] the data aren't convincing" — [Examine.com Tongkat ali](https://examine.com/supplements/tongkat-ali/)
- Fenugreek: both pivotal Testofen trials (Steels 2011; Rao 2016) share authors and the branded extract; the 2011 trial was reported to be partly funded by a commercial partner — [NutraIngredients-USA 2011](https://www.nutraingredients-usa.com/Research/Fenugreek-extract-shows-male-libido-boost-Human-RCT); Steels 2011 enrolled men without ED and did not show a testosterone rise — [Steels 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21312304/); the Furosap® study (100 men, 500 mg/day, 12 weeks) was open-label single-arm, with "reflex erection score" and libido improvements and no adverse events — [Sankhwar et al., J Am Nutr Assoc 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34694954/); a 2025 clinical review questions the clinical implications of ashwagandha and fenugreek "testosterone-boosting" supplements — [Skrzypiec-Spring et al., Adv Clin Exp Med 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38628109/)
- Ashwagandha: sexual-function data in men come from two 8-week trials (n=50 and n=76) in men with low desire or healthy men, not men with diagnosed ED — [Chauhan 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35873404/), [Khanna 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41766918/); the hormone meta-analysis found a modest testosterone increase (+57 ng/dL) with high heterogeneity — [Fornalik 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41740946/)
- Icariin/Epimedium: human evidence absent; a commercial ingredient page states current evidence is limited to preclinical studies and one phase-1 safety/PK study in 24 healthy adults — [Hermetica ingredient page (commercial source, low reliability)](https://ingredients.hermeticasuperfoods.com/ingredients/icariin-beta-d-glucopyranoside); Drugs.com: "clinical trials are lacking to provide information regarding adverse reactions" — [Drugs.com Horny Goat Weed](https://Drugs.com/npp/horny-goat-weed.html); a 2025 meta-analysis of a multi-herb Chinese formula (Compound Xuanju capsules) added to PDE5 inhibitors reported improved EF scores but is not an icariin-alone study — [Frontiers in Pharmacology 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/pharmacology/articles/10.3389/fphar.2025.1537789/pdf)
- Saffron: trials are small and mostly Iranian: 20-man uncontrolled 10-day pilot (increased number/duration of erectile events) — [Shamsa et al., Phytomedicine 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19427775/); topical saffron gel RCT in 50 married diabetic men (IIEF-15 gains vs none on placebo; authors noted small sample) — [Mohammadzadeh-Moghadam et al. 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25948674/); a 2022 double-blind trial (15 mg twice daily, 6 weeks) reported a +6.14-point erectile-function improvement superior to placebo — [Journal of Herbal Medicine 2022 via search summary](https://peeref.com/works/24941160); dedicated saffron meta-analyses exist — [Maleki-Saghooni 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29881706/), [Ranjbar 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31516855/) — but Borrelli 2018 (3 RCTs, n=397) rated saffron results "mixed" — [Borrelli 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29633089/)
- Ginkgo: as of 2008 "there has been only one placebo-controlled trial of GBE for antidepressant-induced sexual dysfunction (Kang 2002)" and it was negative; Meston 2008 studied women only — [Meston et al., Arch Sex Behav 2008 (PDF)](https://springerlink.bibliotecabuap.elogim.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s10508-008-9316-2.pdf); Wheatley 2004 was also negative — [Wheatley 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15378664/)
- Butea superba: Wikipedia states no placebo-controlled studies support aphrodisiac activity — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butea_superba); Ho & Tan 2011 describe human RCTs as scarce — [Curr Urol Rep 2011](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11934-011-0217-x); the sildenafil-comparison study's positive phase was explained by PDE5i adulteration confirmed by the supplier — [Cortés-González 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19624593/)

### Inferences
- The strength-of-evidence hierarchy is distorted by proprietary-extract sponsorship: Tribestan, Physta, Testofen and KSM-66 data largely come from manufacturer-linked trials, and independent replication is rare.
- Saffron's only large, independent-looking trial was negative, so the positive small RCTs (SSRI-induced ED; 6-week blinded trial) should be weighted as hypothesis-generating.

### Gaps
- Funding statements for Rao 2016, Kamenov 2017, Khanna 2026 and Chauhan 2022 are not in the abstracts retrieved; conflict-of-interest detail is from secondary commentary only.
- The 2023 maca meta-analysis (Journal of Men's Health) could not be opened beyond its DOAJ record.

## Key question 4: Documented safety issues (adverse events, liver injury, interactions, regulatory actions, hormonal effects)

### Takeaway
Yohimbine carries the clearest documented harm profile (tachycardia, hypertension, anxiety, arrhythmia, seizures; mislabelled pharmaceutical doses; EU/US restrictions), ashwagandha has an established hepatotoxicity signal (LiverTox likelihood score B/C; Denmark/Sweden bans on thyroid/sex-hormone grounds), Tongkat ali and Tribulus have isolated liver/kidney case reports, ginseng has an RCT-proven warfarin interaction, and adulteration with hidden PDE5 inhibitors is a cross-cutting problem (441 FDA notifications).

### Cited Findings
- Yohimbine, product quality: of 49 US brands, yohimbine per serving ranged from none detected to 12.1 mg; 39% (19/49) lacked the co-alkaloids rauwolscine/corynanthine, implying highly processed or synthetic yohimbine; only 22% (11/49) listed a quantity and most of those were inaccurate (actual 23%-147% of label); 18% gave no adverse-effect information; only 4.1% (2/49) gave both accurate quantity and adverse-effect information — [Cohen et al., Drug Test Anal 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26391406/)
- Yohimbine, poison-centre data (California, 2000-2006): 238 cases; prevalence rose from 1.8 to 8.0 per 10,000 adult exposures; 98.7% involved herbal (not prescription) products; 27.7% used for sexual enhancement; GI distress 46%, tachycardia 43%, anxiety/agitation 33%, hypertension 25%; severe outcomes OR 5.81 (95% CI 4.43-7.64) and need for healthcare-facility management OR 2.35 vs average exposures — [Kearney et al., Ann Pharmacother 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20442348/)
- Yohimbine, NCCIH (updated May 2025): associated with cardiac arrhythmia, blood-pressure problems, heart attacks and seizures; "restricted or banned in many countries"; "illegal in the United States to market an over-the-counter product containing yohimbine as a treatment for erectile dysfunction without getting approval from the FDA"; do not combine with MAOIs or tricyclics; unsafe in pregnancy/breastfeeding — [NCCIH Yohimbe](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/yohimbe)
- Yohimbine, EU: EFSA (2013) could not establish a daily intake that avoids health concerns and noted theoretical maximum intake from supplements may exceed the maximum approved medicinal dose — [EFSA Journal 2013](https://doaj.org/article/0e8e6060dee34fcfaff20a94b3c46290); the Commission then placed yohimbe bark and preparations in Annex III of Regulation 1925/2006 via Regulation (EU) 2015/403 — [Commission Regulation (EU) 2015/403](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2015/403); trade press reported a survey finding these substances not allowed in food supplements in the vast majority of EU member states — [NutraIngredients 2013](https://www.nutraingredients.com/Article/2013/07/26/EFSA-non-opinion-leaves-yohimbe-dead-in-the-water-in-Europe); Mayo: "agitation, increased blood pressure, fast or irregular heartbeat, heart attack and seizures ... Do not use yohimbe without a healthcare professional's guidance" — [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/erectile-dysfunction/in-depth/erectile-dysfunction-herbs/art-20044394)
- Ashwagandha, liver: LiverTox describes it as "recently ... implicated in cases of clinically apparent liver injury" — [LiverTox: Ashwagandha (PubMed record)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31643854/); the current LiverTox page rates likelihood score B ("likely cause"), an older 2019 version C ("probable cause"); injury typically appears 2-12 weeks after starting, usually cholestatic or mixed with jaundice and pruritus, mostly mild-moderate and self-limited within 1-4 months, but "fatal cases due to acute liver failure or acute-on-chronic liver failure have been described"; mislabelling/adulteration of multi-ingredient products is a confounder; withanolides are suspected — [LiverTox NBK548536](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK548536/) (summarised via search; page itself returned errors to the scraper)
- Ashwagandha, regulatory: Denmark's DTU (2020) concluded a safe lower intake could not be set because root extracts "can have a negative effect on thyroid and sex hormones"; Denmark banned ashwagandha in supplements, Sweden applied the Danish assessment, Finland considered a similar ban; industry and some academics disputed the botanical verification and plant parts used — [NutraIngredients 2023](https://nutraingredients.com/Article/2023/05/17/Growing-concerns-over-safety-of-ashwagandha-in-EU-member-states), [NutraIngredients 2024](https://nutraingredients.com/Article/2024/08/01/U.S.-experts-weigh-in-on-European-ashwagandha-concerns); a secondary source reports Australia's TGA calling liver injury "very rare" with seven implicating reports as of February 2024 (not verified against the TGA notice) — [Healthy Male (Australia)](https://healthymale.org.au/health-article/ashwagandha-supplements-testosterone-men)
- Ashwagandha, thyroid: pooled RCT data show T4 increased modestly (MD +0.61 µg/dL) with no TSH/T3 change — [Fornalik 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41740946/)
- Tongkat ali, liver: LiverTox (2024) notes "reports of clinically apparent liver injury attributed to tongkat ali in body builders have recently appeared" and cites one case — [LiverTox: Tongkat Ali (PubMed record)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39527684/) / [NBK609015](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK609015/); the index case was a 47-year-old man with jaundice and elevated enzymes shortly after starting Tongkat ali, improving on discontinuation, described as "the first reported case of its kind" — [Kaliounji et al., Cureus 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38646387/); Examine: generally well tolerated (mild GI effects), one acute liver injury case, contamination with lead and mercury in some Malaysian products, undisclosed sildenafil analogues found in some products, possible reduction of propranolol levels, possible CYP1A2/CYP2A6 interactions, avoid in pregnancy/lactation, caution for WADA-tested athletes — [Examine.com Tongkat ali](https://examine.com/supplements/tongkat-ali/); a 6-week trial in recreational athletes found no change in liver/renal function or urinary T/E ratio — [Chen et al., Int J Prev Med 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25013692/)
- Tribulus, liver/kidney case reports: 46-year-old man after 2 months of daily Tribulus: bilirubin peak 48 mg/dL, creatinine 7.1, biopsy consistent with drug-induced liver injury, treated with plasmapheresis, recovered (bilirubin 1.1 at follow-up) — [Mohy-Ud-Din & Jonassaint, ACG Case Rep J 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38328764/); young male with severe hyperbilirubinaemia then acute renal failure with bile casts — [Ryan et al., Clin Nephrol 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25295577/); Iranian man with seizure, very high aminotransferases and creatinine after 2 days of herbal water, resolving on discontinuation — [Talasaz et al., Nephrol Dial Transplant 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20667992/); 43-year-old man requiring dialysis for AKI after self-administered Tribulus — [Freidoon et al. 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42234880/); a fatal case of toxin-induced liver failure in a 31-year-old woman drinking Tribulus tea for 2-3 months — [Ataee & Dadpour, Asia Pac J Med Toxicol 2020](https://apjmt.mums.ac.ir/article_15318.html); a published rebuttal questioned causality in the nephrotoxicity report — [Schmidt et al. 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21712491/); LiverTox's Tribulus entry concludes extracts "have not been linked convincingly to instances of clinically apparent liver injury" at typical doses, suggests the Ryan case was more likely anabolic-steroid related, and notes well-documented hepatotoxicity in grazing animals (preclinical/veterinary) — [LiverTox: Tribulus NBK583201](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK583201/) (via search summary)
- Ginseng interactions and adverse effects: American ginseng reduced warfarin's effect in a randomized double-blind trial of 20 healthy volunteers (peak INR difference -0.19, 95% CI -0.36 to -0.07; P=0.0012; warfarin AUC and peak level also reduced); "when prescribing warfarin, physicians should ask patients about ginseng use" — [Yuan et al., Ann Intern Med 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15238367/); Mayo: Panax ginseng "appears to be safe when used for up to 6 months. Insomnia, headaches and vertigo are common side effects" — [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/erectile-dysfunction/in-depth/erectile-dysfunction-herbs/art-20044394); Examine: well tolerated below 3 g/day; "ginseng abuse syndrome" (nervousness, hypertension, insomnia, GI symptoms, skin eruptions) reported with 3-15 g/day long-term but from low-quality evidence; caution with QTc prolongation/arrhythmia; possible hypoglycaemia with glucose-lowering drugs; avoid in pregnancy; widespread adulteration/species substitution and some products spiked with sildenafil — [Examine.com Panax ginseng](https://examine.com/supplements/panax-ginseng/); Cochrane: adverse events RR 1.45 (95% CI 0.69-3.03), i.e. 39 more per 1000 (95% CI 27 fewer to 174 more) — [Lee 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33871063/)
- Fenugreek: LiverTox likelihood score E (unlikely cause of liver injury); no US herbal-supplement liver-injury cases attributed to it — [LiverTox: Fenugreek NBK548826](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK548826/); a 2026 scoping review of adverse effects lists hypoglycaemia, possible hypokalaemia, allergic reactions, maple-syrup odour, and medication interactions — [PMC12858439](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12858439/); potential interaction with warfarin/antiplatelets due to coumarin-like constituents is a cited concern — [NCCIH Fenugreek](https://nccih.nih.gov/health/fenugreek)
- Maca: Gonzales trials showed no change in reproductive hormones at 1.5-3 g/day — [Gonzales 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12525260/); an online-market analysis (203 websites) linked yohimbine, maca, horny goat weed and ginkgo products to reports of mood changes, anxiety, hallucinations and addictive behaviours — [Corazza et al., Biomed Res Int 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25025070/)
- Horny goat weed: case report of tachyarrhythmia and hypomania in a 66-year-old man with cardiovascular disease after two weeks of a product (Partin & Pushkin 2004), causality unclear because the product may have been multi-ingredient — [MSKCC Epimedium monograph](https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/integrative-medicine/herbs/epimedium); 2025 case of severe muscle spasms with raised CK and creatinine — [Cureus 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12182873/); Mayo lists dizziness, dry mouth, nosebleed, thirst, vomiting and possible effects on heart or breathing — [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/erectile-dysfunction/in-depth/erectile-dysfunction-herbs/art-20044394)
- Saffron: toxic at total daily doses >=5 g; caution with antiplatelet/anticoagulant drugs — [CSIRO/Hepatology Communications review](https://publish.csiro.au/hc/Fulltext/HC22067); up to 1.5 g/day thought safe, 10-20 g may be fatal — [Drugs.com Saffron](https://www.drugs.com/npp/saffron.html); in the 346-man crossover and 36-man SSRI trials, side-effect frequency did not differ from comparator/placebo — [Safarinejad 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20520621/), [Modabbernia 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22552758/)
- Ginkgo: Mayo: "Rarely, ginkgo may raise the risk of bleeding and irregular heartbeats" — [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/erectile-dysfunction/in-depth/erectile-dysfunction-herbs/art-20044394)
- Butea superba, hormonal: a case report of hyperandrogenaemia after ingestion — [Chaiyasit & Wiwanitkit, Indian J Endocrinol Metab 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22629537/); rat study of androgen disruption (testosterone/LH) warned of "possible adverse risk of over-dose" — PRECLINICAL — [Cherdshewasart et al., Maturitas 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18554827/)
- Adulteration (cross-cutting): FDA's sexual-enhancement notification database listed 441 products as of 15 Oct 2026 and warns such products "are likely to be contaminated with dangerous hidden ingredients" and that the list "covers only a small fraction of the contaminated products on the market" — [FDA Sexual Enhancement and Energy Product Notifications](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/medication-health-fraud-notifications/sexual-enhancement-and-energy-product-notifications); NCCIH: "Many supplements promoted for ED and sexual enhancement—sometimes called 'herbal Viagra'—have been found to be tainted with drug ingredients", and some interact with nitrates causing dangerous hypotension — [NCCIH ED page](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/erectile-dysfunctionsexual-enhancement)

### Inferences
- For daily, long-term use, the botanicals with the most reassuring human safety data are ginseng (<3 g/day, up to 6 months) and fenugreek (LiverTox E), with the caveats of warfarin/antidiabetic interactions; ashwagandha and Tongkat ali carry a real (if rare) idiosyncratic hepatotoxicity risk; yohimbine is not appropriate for unsupervised daily use.
- The single biggest safety risk for consumers may not be the herb itself but undeclared PDE5 inhibitors, which would be dangerous with nitrates and would also explain some "herbal" successes (Butea superba).

### Gaps
- Could not directly open the LiverTox ashwagandha or Tribulus pages (likelihood scores and case counts rely on search-engine summaries).
- The Australian TGA ashwagandha safety advisory was not verified from the primary TGA source.
- No incidence estimates exist for herb-induced liver injury per user; all hepatotoxicity data are case reports.

## Key question 5: Daily doses and standardizations actually studied

### Takeaway
Studied regimens are narrow: KRG 1.4-3 g/day (900-1,000 mg three times daily), maca 1.5-3 g/day (2.4 g/day in the ED trial), Tribulus 800-1,500 mg/day extract (Tribestan standardized to >=45% furostanol saponins), Tongkat ali 100-600 mg/day of a water-soluble extract (Physta), Testofen fenugreek 600 mg/day, ashwagandha root extract 300 mg twice daily, saffron 30 mg/day (15 mg twice daily), ginkgo 240 mg/day, and yohimbine in pharmaceutical (not supplement) doses.

### Cited Findings
- Ginseng: Examine states KRG for erectile health is "commonly administered at a dosage of 3 grams daily, divided into three doses of 1 gram each"; general standardized extracts (4-10% ginsenosides) 200-400 mg/day — [Examine.com Panax ginseng](https://examine.com/supplements/panax-ginseng/); trial doses: 900 mg three times daily (Hong 2002) — [PubMed 12394711](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12394711/); 1,400 mg/day ginseng berry extract x 8 weeks (Choi 2013) — [PubMed 23254461](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23254461/); Cochrane trials ran <=12 weeks — [Lee 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33871063/)
- Maca: 2,400 mg/day dry extract x 12 weeks (Zenico 2009) — [PubMed 19260845](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19260845/); 1,500 or 3,000 mg/day gelatinized maca x 12 weeks (Gonzales 2002/2003) — [PubMed 12472620](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12472620/); 3.0 g/day (not 1.5 g) was effective in SSRI-induced dysfunction (Dording 2008) — [PubMed 18801111](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18801111/); "most human trials have used approximately 3 grams per day of maca powder" — [Bloomer 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42680584/)
- Tribulus: Tribestan 250 mg tablets (35-45:1 extract; >=112.5 mg furostanol saponins each), 3 x 2 tablets/day for 12 weeks = 1,500 mg extract (~675 mg saponins) — [Kamenov 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28364864/); 800 mg/day x 30 days in the negative Santos trial — [Santos 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24630840/)
- Tongkat ali: Physta® water-soluble extract 100 mg/day (Chinnappan, 12 weeks), 200 mg/day (Tambi, 1 month), 300 mg/day (Ismail, George, 12 weeks), 400 mg/day (Henkel), 600 mg/day (Chan, 2 weeks) — [Leisegang 2022 table](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9415500/); Examine: "standard dosage ... for testosterone is 100-600 mg/day. For sexual health and libido, a daily dose of 200-400 mg seems to be effective," typically as a standardized water-soluble extract — [Examine.com Tongkat ali](https://examine.com/supplements/tongkat-ali/)
- Fenugreek: Testofen 600 mg/day (two tablets) x 6 weeks (Steels) and x 12 weeks (Rao) — [Steels 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21312304/), [Rao 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26791805/); Furosap® (20% protodioscin) 500 mg/day x 12 weeks — [Sankhwar 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34694954/)
- Ashwagandha: standardized root extract 300 mg twice daily (600 mg/day) x 8 weeks in both male sexual-function RCTs — [Chauhan 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35873404/), [Khanna 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41766918/); the hormone meta-analysis suggested possible dose-response by withanolide content — [Fornalik 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41740946/)
- Saffron: 30 mg twice daily x 12 weeks (negative, Safarinejad) — [PubMed 20520621](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20520621/); 15 mg twice daily x 4 weeks (positive, Modabbernia) — [PubMed 22552758](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22552758/); 30 mg/day is the standard dose across depression trials with favourable short-term tolerability — [CSIRO/Hepatology Communications review](https://publish.csiro.au/hc/Fulltext/HC22067)
- Ginkgo: 240 mg/day x 12 weeks (Wheatley) — [PubMed 15378664](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15378664/); 60-240 mg/day, mean 209 mg/day (Cohen & Bartlik open trial) — [PubMed 9611693](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9611693/)
- Yohimbine: supplement content ranged from undetectable to 12.1 mg per serving, often unlabelled — [Cohen 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26391406/); NCCIH notes supplement yohimbe "may not work like the prescription medication that contains yohimbine" — [NCCIH Yohimbe](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/yohimbe)
- Butea superba: 100 mg "natural health product" dose in the sildenafil-comparison study (adulterated batch) — [Cortés-González 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19624593/); crude tuber preparation, dose not specified in the 2003 abstract — [Cherdshewasart 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12937809/)

### Inferences
- No trial of any of these botanicals for sexual outcomes exceeded about 6 months (most 4-12 weeks), so "daily long-term use" safety is extrapolated, not demonstrated.
- Marketed blends typically under-dose relative to trial regimens (Petre 2023: 80% of Italian products had no expected efficacy), so consumers rarely receive the studied dose.

### Gaps
- Standardization markers (e.g., ginsenoside %, eurycomanone content of Physta, withanolide % of KSM-66) are not reported in the abstracts retrieved.

## Key question 6: What do Examine.com, NIH/NCCIH, Mayo Clinic, and urology guideline bodies (AUA, EAU) say?

### Takeaway
NCCIH states no complementary approach has been shown safe and effective for ED; Mayo rates ginseng and L-arginine as "studied in people, positive results, generally safe" but warns against yohimbe and unproven herbs; the EAU guideline (2025) gives a weak recommendation that daily L-arginine or ginseng may be used in mild ED for men who refuse drugs, after counselling that improvement "could be mild"; the AUA guideline does not endorse herbal products, and AUA commentary notes limited efficacy data and lack of independent quality testing.

### Cited Findings
- NCCIH: "No complementary health approaches have been shown to be safe and effective for sexual enhancement or treating ED. Safety is a serious concern"; "There is no definite evidence that any herbal products are safe or effective for erectile dysfunction ... the amount of research on each herb is small, and much of the research has been done in animals rather than people"; tainted "herbal Viagra" products and nitrate interactions are flagged — [NCCIH: Erectile Dysfunction/Sexual Enhancement](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/erectile-dysfunctionsexual-enhancement), [NCCIH: 4 things to know](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/tips/4-things-to-know-about-dietary-supplements-marketed-for-sexual-enhancement)
- Mayo Clinic (updated Jan 2025): table rates L-arginine ("some research shows that taking high doses improves ED ... Don't take L-arginine with sildenafil"), Panax ginseng ("may improve sexual function in people with ED ... safe when used for up to 6 months"), propionyl-L-carnitine (with sildenafil), DHEA (early research; long-term use linked to some cancers), yohimbe (small studies but "dangerous side effects"), ginkgo ("not enough proof"), horny goat weed ("not enough evidence"); "Don't use supplements for ED that lack proven benefits and pose a potential risk for harm"; FDA "has banned many of these products" — [Mayo Clinic: Dietary supplements for ED](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/erectile-dysfunction/in-depth/erectile-dysfunction-herbs/art-20044394)
- EAU Guidelines on Sexual and Reproductive Health, section 5.6.9 "Herbal medicine and natural supplements": "exponential growth in the market ... but with very little available evidence of robust scientific data to support their efficacy and safety. A Cochrane review showed that ginseng may only have trivial effects on EF ..."; an 18-trial meta-analysis of antioxidants showed L-arginine increased IIEF-EF by 2.74 (95% CI 1.67-3.81) vs placebo with no difference in satisfaction; "this small improvement may not be perceived as significant by patients"; recommendation table: "Use supplements with L-arginine or ginseng daily in men with mild ED who refuse pharmacological treatment after counselling them that the improvement of EF could be mild" — strength rating: Weak — [EAU Guidelines: Management of Erectile Dysfunction](https://uroweb.org/guidelines/sexual-and-reproductive-health/chapter/management-of-erectile-dysfunction)
- AUA 2018 ED guideline covers PDE5 inhibitors, vacuum devices, intraurethral alprostadil, intracavernosal injection and penile implants and contains no recommendation for herbal supplements — [AUA Guideline summary (UroToday)](https://www.urotoday.com/recent-abstracts/men-s-health/erectile-dysfunction/104021-erectile-dysfunction-aua-guideline.html); AUA News 2022: "most herbal supplements provide minimal harm; however, there are limited efficacy data," and "most of the sexual supplements available do not utilize any independent testing for product quality" — [AUA News, Aug 2022 (via search summary)](https://auanews.net/issues/articles/2022/august-2022/aua2022-reflections-the-safety-and-efficacy-of-sexual-supplements); a review notes phytotherapy "is lacking in the EAU and most of other major Guidelines due to product heterogeneity and lack of standardization" — [Springer review 2020](https://springerlink.bibliotecabuap.elogim.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s11934-020-01023-9.pdf)
- French AFU/SFMS evidence synthesis: strongest phytotherapy signal was traditional Chinese herbal medicine combined with tadalafil, but formulas were non-standardized and heterogeneous, so it is "not a validated herbal adjunct for routine use"; clinicians should "prioritize validated erectile dysfunction care" — [UroToday summary of AFU/SFMS synthesis](https://www.urotoday.com/recent-abstracts/men-s-health/erectile-dysfunction/170833-phytotherapies-and-complementary-therapies-for-erectile-dysfunction-a-focused-secondary-synthesis-and-critical-clinical-interpretation-of-the-afu-sfms-evidence-base-beyond-the-abstract.html)
- Examine.com: Panax ginseng "appears to be effective for mood, immunity, and cognition, but is subpar for erectile function, testosterone, and exercise performance" (page description); erectile dysfunction database entry lists 2 outcomes from 9 studies — [Examine.com Panax ginseng](https://examine.com/supplements/panax-ginseng/); Tongkat ali: "while some evidence exists for [testosterone] in men with low testosterone and the mechanisms are plausible, the data aren't convincing"; ED entry based on 2 studies — [Examine.com Tongkat ali](https://examine.com/supplements/tongkat-ali/)
- FDA: maintains a public database of 441 sexual-enhancement/energy products with hidden drug ingredients (as of 15 Oct 2026), warning that such products "pose a serious health risk and are not guaranteed to work" — [FDA notifications](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/medication-health-fraud-notifications/sexual-enhancement-and-energy-product-notifications)

### Inferences
- The EAU's weak recommendation for daily ginseng (or L-arginine) in mild ED among men who decline drugs is the only guideline-level endorsement of a botanical, and it is explicitly framed around trivial-to-small expected benefit.
- Independent reference bodies (NCCIH, Mayo, Examine) consistently flag product quality (adulteration, mislabelling, dose variability) as the dominant practical safety concern for daily "herbal ED" use.
