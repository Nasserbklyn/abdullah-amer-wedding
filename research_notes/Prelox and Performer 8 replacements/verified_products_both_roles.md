# Verified products: single products spanning both roles

**Scope:** Single bottles or single herbs with some evidence in both the vascular and the drive role, plus multi-ingredient men's formulas that were checked and rejected.

**Method:** Each product was checked by a verification agent (label and directions, certification in the certifier's own database, regulatory record, dose against human trials, safety, interactions, who should avoid it, stop signals) and then audited by two challenge agents, one on certification and label claims and one on safety and evidence. Captures date from 7–8 November 2026. Records are listed in the order of `reports/Prelox and Performer 8 replacements.md`.

## Takeaway

- No bottle covers both roles at trial doses with a drug-screening certification.
- Korean red ginseng has the best both-role evidence (IIEF-EF +3.52 across nine RCTs, low certainty) but is best run as a separate trial after the two-product plan.
- GNC Men's ArginMax prints 3 g arginine but under-doses its botanicals; it is uncertified. The workflow filed it under the vascular role; the report ranks it with the both-role products.
- Sunday Natural Libido M Erect, Irwin Naturals Steel-Libido RED and Nugenix Total-T are on the avoid list.

## Gaps

- FDA's warning-letter, recall and tainted-product pages and the Health Canada, TGA and MHRA databases were blocked or unreachable for most products, so a missing regulatory action means "not checked", not "clean".
- Several certifier pages were seen only through search-engine copies; the report marks these as indirect, and the live page must be checked against the bottle's lot number.
- Informed Sport and NSF Certified for Sport do not publish whether their panels cover PDE5 drugs (sildenafil, tadalafil and analogues); BSCG's published menu does.
- No prices or costs were collected, by design.

- **Evidence summary:** Ginseng has the most erectile-function RCTs of any herb, but the effect is small:
- Cochrane 2021 (9 RCTs, 587 men with mild-moderate ED, up to 12 weeks): IIEF erectile domain +3.52 points (low certainty), IIEF-5 +2.39 (moderate certainty), self-reported ability to have intercourse RR 2.55 (low certainty). Its conclusion: ginseng 'may only have trivial effects' on erectile function [https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33871063/].
- Borrelli 2018 pooled five ginseng single-ingredient RCTs (SMD 0.43 on IIEF-5) [https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29633089/].
- Ho 2025 (14 herbal RCTs) found ginseng improved erectile function, orgasm, desire, overall satisfaction and testosterone [https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41696741/].
The EAU gives a weak recommendation for daily ginseng (or arginine) in mild ED [https://uroweb.org/guidelines/sexual-and-reproductive-health/chapter/management-of-erectile-dysfunction]. NCCIH says oral Asian ginseng seems to improve sexual function in ED [https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/asian-ginseng].
Most positive trials used Korean red ginseng, not a generic Panax extract like this one. Its desire and testosterone signals are weaker than the erectile signal, and these reviews contain no stress or cortisol data.

**Safety risks**

- Insomnia is the most common side effect. Uncommon reports include severe rash, liver damage and severe allergic reactions. Asian ginseng may worsen autoimmune disorders and interfere with blood clotting [https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/asian-ginseng].
- Other effects: dry mouth, tachycardia, nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea and nervousness [https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/integrative-medicine/herbs/ginseng-asian]; insomnia, headaches and vertigo [https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/erectile-dysfunction/in-depth/erectile-dysfunction-herbs/art-20044394].
- May lower blood sugar, especially with insulin or sulfonylureas [https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/asian-ginseng; https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/integrative-medicine/herbs/ginseng-asian].
- Short-term use of up to 6 months appears safe for most people; questions have been raised about long-term safety [https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/asian-ginseng].
- High intakes: a 'ginseng abuse syndrome' (nervousness, high blood pressure, insomnia, GI symptoms, skin eruptions) has been reported at 3-15 g/day long term, though the evidence is low quality. Examine also advises caution with QTc prolongation or arrhythmia [https://examine.com/supplements/panax-ginseng/].
- Activated the estrogen receptor in breast-cancer cells in the laboratory [https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/integrative-medicine/herbs/ginseng-asian]. ConsumerLab advises against use with breast cancer [https://www.consumerlab.com/reviews/ginseng-supplements/ginseng/].
- Dose: a 1 g serving is about five times a typical extract dose, and the label's maximum of 2 g/day has no trial safety data. This is my inference from ConsumerLab's dosing guide [https://www.consumerlab.com/reviews/ginseng-supplements/ginseng/].
- Identity and ginsenoside content were confirmed only for a 2021 sample, and the product is not screened for drugs. Across the ginseng category, species substitution and products spiked with sildenafil have been reported [https://examine.com/supplements/panax-ginseng/].

**Interactions**

- Nitrates (nitroglycerin, isosorbide): NCCIH and MSKCC document no interaction, but the combination has not been studied; ask a doctor first [https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/asian-ginseng; https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/integrative-medicine/herbs/ginseng-asian].
- PDE5 inhibitors (sildenafil, tadalafil): no interaction documented, and no trial has compared or combined ginseng with a PDE5 inhibitor [https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33871063/].
- Antihypertensives: MSKCC lists no interaction, but ginseng can cause tachycardia and, at high intakes, raised blood pressure, so monitor blood pressure [https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/integrative-medicine/herbs/ginseng-asian; https://examine.com/supplements/panax-ginseng/].
- Anticoagulants and antiplatelets: American ginseng reduced warfarin's effect in a randomized trial (peak INR -0.19) [https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15238367/]. Studies of Asian ginseng with anticoagulants are mixed; it may increase bleeding risk and may raise aspirin bioavailability [https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/integrative-medicine/herbs/ginseng-asian].
- Diabetes drugs: may increase the glucose-lowering effect of insulin and sulfonylureas [https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/integrative-medicine/herbs/ginseng-asian].
- MAOIs (for example phenelzine): manic-like symptoms reported [https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/integrative-medicine/herbs/ginseng-asian]. SSRIs: no interaction documented in the sources reviewed.
- Thyroid drugs and sedatives: NCCIH and MSKCC document no interaction.
- Other drugs: certain ginsenosides induce CYP3A4 (human relevance uncertain), and there are case reports of liver injury with imatinib and raised raltegravir levels [https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/integrative-medicine/herbs/ginseng-asian]. Caffeine or other stimulants may add to insomnia and nervousness (my inference from the side-effect profile).

**Who should avoid it**

- Men on warfarin or other anticoagulants or antiplatelets, or with a bleeding disorder, unless their doctor monitors them [https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15238367/; https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/integrative-medicine/herbs/ginseng-asian].
- Men with diabetes on insulin or sulfonylureas, unless blood glucose is monitored [https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/integrative-medicine/herbs/ginseng-asian].
- Anyone taking an MAOI [https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/integrative-medicine/herbs/ginseng-asian].
- People with autoimmune disease [https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/asian-ginseng].
- Men with insomnia, anxiety, uncontrolled high blood pressure, arrhythmia or QT prolongation [https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/integrative-medicine/herbs/ginseng-asian; https://examine.com/supplements/panax-ginseng/].
- People taking imatinib or raltegravir [https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/integrative-medicine/herbs/ginseng-asian].
- Anyone with an estrogen-sensitive cancer [https://www.consumerlab.com/reviews/ginseng-supplements/ginseng/].
- Men with new, unexplained ED who have not had a cardiovascular check [https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16854949/].

**Stop signals**

- Trouble sleeping, nervousness, palpitations or a fast heartbeat.
- Headache or vertigo that persists [https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/erectile-dysfunction/in-depth/erectile-dysfunction-herbs/art-20044394].
- Rash, swelling or difficulty breathing: stop and get urgent care [https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/asian-ginseng].
- Yellow skin or eyes, dark urine, itching or upper-right abdominal pain; liver damage is uncommon but has been reported [https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/asian-ginseng].
- Unusual bruising or bleeding, or an INR change on warfarin.
- Shakiness, sweating or confusion (low blood sugar) if you take diabetes medicines.
- Agitation or an unusually elevated mood, especially with an antidepressant.
- No improvement after 8-12 weeks, the usual trial length [https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2561113/].
- About 6 months of continuous use: pause and review, because longer use is less studied [https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/asian-ginseng].

### Avoid list: Irwin Naturals — Steel-Libido RED (Magnum Blood-Flow liquid soft-gels, 75 or 150)

- **Verifier verdict:** avoid — Its 1.5 g of citrulline matches the only citrulline ED pilot, but it also contains a drug-like dose of L-dopa that interacts with MAOIs and antipsychotics, plus a stimulant and ginkgo. Some amounts are hidden and it is uncertified. A certified plain citrulline gives the same 1.5 g without these risks.
- **Product as verified:** Irwin Naturals — Steel-Libido RED (Magnum Blood-Flow liquid soft-gels, 75 or 150)
- **Product page:** https://www.vitacost.com/products/irwin-naturals-steel-libido-red-magnum-blood-flow-150-liquid-soft-gels-48620
- **Where sold:** US: GNC, Rite Aid, Target, Vitacost, Swanson and supermarket chains (Fry's, Randalls, Acme). UK, EU and Gulf: not verified.
- **Label verified:** no. Irwin Naturals' own GNC listing (gnc.com 269335) and the Rite Aid, Vitacost and Swanson listings, all read through search-result text on 8 Nov 2026. The listings disagree with each other. No manufacturer label panel or DSLD record was opened.
- **Proprietary blend:** yes
- **Directions and daily amounts:** Serving size is 4 liquid soft-gels; servings per container are listed as 18, 21 or 37, and the exact directions were not captured. Per 4 soft-gels: L-citrulline 1,500 mg; MCT oil 1,000 mg; pumpkin seed oil 1,000 mg; velvet bean (Mucuna pruriens) extract 400 mg standardized to 15% L-dopa (about 60 mg L-dopa); CoQ10 50 mg; superoxide dismutase 50 mg; BioPerine complex (piperine) 6 mg. Ginkgo extract (24% flavone glycosides, 6% terpene lactones) is 120 mg on the Rite Aid listing only, and theobromine is 92 mg on one listing only. Ginseng (species not stated) and L-theanine are named with no amounts.

After 1 month, the erection hardness score rose from 3 to 4 in 50% on citrulline versus 8.3% on placebo [https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21195829/]. Fewer than 4 soft-gels falls below that dose. Ginkgo at 120 mg is half of 240 mg/day, a dose that was itself no better than placebo [https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15378664/]. Mucuna (60 mg L-dopa), CoQ10, superoxide dismutase, pumpkin seed oil and MCT have no ED trials in this project's research. None of the other ingredients has ED evidence at these doses.

**Safety risks**

- About 60 mg of L-dopa from Mucuna: the same molecule as the Parkinson's drug levodopa, with drug-like effects and interactions. It has not been studied for ED.
- It contains theobromine, a caffeine-like methylxanthine (92 mg on one listing), and piperine, a bioavailability enhancer that can raise blood levels of some drugs (general pharmacology; not verified this session).
- Ginkgo: dizziness, digestive symptoms and headache; 'rarely, ginkgo may raise the risk of bleeding and irregular heartbeats' (Mayo). Harvard links it to seizures and bleeding [https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/erectile-dysfunction/in-depth/erectile-dysfunction-herbs/art-20044394] [https://www.health.harvard.edu/mens-health/beware-of-erectile-dysfunction-scams].
- Citrulline lowers blood pressure, by an estimated 4.1/2.08 to 7.54/3.77 mmHg [https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6683098/]. NIH ODS says there isn't enough research to know whether it is safe long term [https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/ExerciseAndAthleticPerformance-Consumer/].
- Some amounts are not disclosed (ginseng, L-theanine) and the listings conflict, so total exposure is uncertain.

**Interactions**

- Nitrates and other vasodilators: L-citrulline 'may potentiate the hypotensive effects of nitrates and vasodilators' (Drugs.com); avoid the combination [https://www.drugs.com/drug-interactions/arginine-with-sildenafil-232-0-2061-0.html?professional=1].
- PDE5 inhibitors: blood-pressure lowering may add up; Drugs.com rates the pairing 'Moderate - monitor'.
- Antihypertensives and alpha-blockers: additive blood-pressure lowering [https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6683098/].
- MAOIs (phenelzine, tranylcypromine; caution with selegiline, rasagiline and linezolid): levodopa labeling contraindicates non-selective MAOIs because of a hypertensive-reaction risk [https://www.drugs.com/pro/sinemet.html, not opened this session]. Ginseng combined with MAOIs 'may cause manic-like symptoms' (MSKCC) [https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/integrative-medicine/herbs/ginseng-asian].
- Antipsychotics, metoclopramide, levodopa/carbidopa and dopamine agonists: the L-dopa may oppose or add to these drugs (inferred from levodopa pharmacology; not verified this session).
- Anticoagulants and antiplatelets: ginkgo occasionally increases bleeding risk (Mayo), and ginseng altered warfarin response in an RCT [https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15238367/].
- Diabetes drugs: ginseng may strengthen the effect of insulin and sulfonylureas (MSKCC).
- Caffeine and other stimulants: theobromine adds to their effect.
- Thyroid drugs, SSRIs and sedatives: no specific data reviewed.

**Who should avoid it**

- Anyone taking MAOIs, antipsychotics, levodopa or dopamine agonists
- Nitrate users
- Men on anticoagulants or antiplatelets
- Men with heart-rhythm problems, uncontrolled blood pressure or anxiety disorders
- Men with Parkinson's disease or psychotic illness
- Men on several blood-pressure drugs

**Stop signals**

- Palpitations, chest pain or a sudden severe headache
- Dizziness or fainting
- Nausea or vomiting, involuntary movements, confusion or hallucinations
- Agitation or insomnia
- Unusual bleeding or bruising
