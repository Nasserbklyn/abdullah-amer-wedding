# Arginine Beats the Blends, Trails Viagra

No daily supplement on the market comes close to Viagra or Cialis, but one narrow category is both safe for most men and modestly effective: a single-ingredient nitric-oxide precursor — **L-arginine at 2.5–6 g/day, or L-citrulline at 1.5–3 g/day** as its better-tolerated proxy — bought from a brand whose lots appear in a drug-screening certification database, optionally paired with a standardized Korean red ginseng extract. The best specific picks today are **Kaged Fermented L-Citrulline** (on Informed Sport's certified-product list, about **$7/month** at 3 g/day) or **NOW Sports Arginine & Citrulline** (also Informed Sport-listed), plus **BulkSupplements Ginseng Root Extract** (1 g at ≥7% ginsenosides, ConsumerLab-approved, roughly **$6–12/month**); a man who wants the largest reported effect and accepts manufacturer-funded evidence can add **Pharma Nord Prelox** (UK/EU, the only Prelox whose label discloses its 80 mg Pycnogenol + 2.8 g arginine aspartate daily dose, about £36/month). The honest size of the benefit is small: placebo-controlled trials put L-arginine at about **+2.7 to +4 points** on the 30-point IIEF erectile-function scale and ginseng at **+3.5**, versus **+7 to +9.7** for on-demand sildenafil and **+5.4** for daily tadalafil 5 mg — one-third to one-half of a drug effect, about equal to six months of aerobic exercise (+2.8) and less than pelvic-floor training with biofeedback (+6.7). Every heavily advertised "male enhancement" blend fails the safety-or-dose test: VigRX Plus and ExtenZe hide ingredient amounts in proprietary blends (ExtenZe drew an FDA hidden-sildenafil notification in 2018), Performer 8, Male Extra and Semenax carry one-sixth to one-twelfth of the arginine dose that worked in trials, Nugenix Total-T under-doses most of its ingredients at $64–70/month, and Boostaro, EndoPeak and Alpha Tonic are ClickBank affiliate funnels sold through deepfake celebrity ads. Independent laboratory surveys find **67–81%** of "herbal" sexual-performance pills contain undeclared sildenafil or tadalafil, FDA's hidden-drug list for this category stood at **441 products** on 7 November 2026, and yohimbe is the one legal ingredient with documented fatalities, so gas-station pills, honey packs, coffees and anything promising results "in 30 minutes" are off the table regardless of what the label says. Two facts should frame the whole decision: FDA-approved generic tadalafil 5 mg daily now costs **$6–14 a month** through Cost Plus Drugs or a GoodRx coupon, cheaper than nearly every supplement blend, and erectile dysfunction precedes coronary events by **two to five years**, so a man with new ED should have blood pressure, lipids, HbA1c and a morning testosterone checked before self-treating with anything.

## Only arginine and ginseng earn a guideline nod, and the honest gain is 3 to 4 IIEF points

Any claim about a supplement has to be read against the drug it is meant to replace. The AUA's systematic review of general-population trials found placebo alone raises the IIEF erectile-function (IIEF-EF) domain by **+1.78 points**, sildenafil by **+9.00** and tadalafil by **+7.82** from baselines near 14 ([AUA 2018 ED Guideline](https://www.auanet.org/documents/guidelines/ed%20website%20final.pdf)). A fixed-dose meta-analysis put the placebo-subtracted gain at **+9.65** for sildenafil 100 mg and **+8.52** for tadalafil 20 mg ([Berner 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16239897/)), and the integrated analysis of 1,913 men on once-daily tadalafil found **+5.4 points over placebo at 5 mg**, with 66% of mild, 48% of moderate and 29% of severe cases returning to the normal range ([Porst 2014](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0302283813010245)). On the Viagra label, 63–82% of men on 25–100 mg reported improved erections versus 24% on placebo ([Viagra prescribing information](https://labeling.pfizer.com/ShowLabeling.aspx?format=PDF&id=652)). The minimal clinically important difference is about 2, 5 and 7 IIEF-EF points for mild, moderate and severe ED ([Psychiatrist.com commentary on Khera 2023](https://www.psychiatrist.com/jcp/aerobic-exercise-for-erectile-dysfunction-rct-data/)). The placebo figure matters doubly: across 63 double-blind PDE5-inhibitor trials the placebo arms improved by a small-to-moderate effect (Hedges g 0.35) ([Stridh 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7084170/)), so any supplement study without a placebo arm — which describes most "clinically studied" marketing claims — overstates the true effect by roughly 2–3 points.

L-arginine is the only supplement ingredient that survives this comparison with a statistically significant effect of its own. In a 2024 network meta-analysis of 15 placebo-controlled RCTs (1,000 men), L-arginine alone was the sole single nutraceutical with significant efficacy, ranking below sildenafil and tadalafil (SUCRA 52% versus 79% and 72%) "against a background of general ineffectiveness of most nutraceutical interventions" ([Barbonetti 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39279185/)). The current European Association of Urology guideline quantifies the gain at **+2.74 IIEF-EF points** (95% CI 1.67–3.81) over placebo with no difference in satisfaction, warns "this small improvement may not be perceived as significant by patients," and issues a *weak* recommendation to "use supplements with L-arginine or ginseng daily in men with mild ED who refuse pharmacological treatment after counselling them that the improvement of EF could be mild" ([EAU Guidelines, Management of ED](https://uroweb.org/guidelines/sexual-and-reproductive-health/chapter/management-of-erectile-dysfunction)). The best single trial is the 98-man double-blind Menafra study: **6 g/day** (2 g three times daily after meals) for three months raised median IIEF-6 from 20 to 24 while placebo was unchanged, cut the share with moderate ED from 31% to 8% and moved 24% to a no-ED score, although the haemodynamic gain was confined to mild-to-moderate rather than severe vasculogenic ED ([Menafra 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40618-021-01704-3)). The 300-man Gallo comparison is the most useful head-to-head: **2.5 g/day arginine gave +3.1 IIEF-EF versus +6.0 for daily tadalafil 5 mg**, yet in mild ED the two were statistically indistinguishable (+5.4 vs +5.7), in severe ED arginine did nothing (+0.1), and adverse events were 11 versus 53 per 100 men ([Gallo 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7261690/)). Dose is decisive: 1.5 g/day for 17 days was no better than placebo (17% vs 20% improved) ([Klotz 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10743698/)), and the pooled odds ratio of 3.37 for improvement in mild-to-moderate ED comes from trials at 1.5–5 g/day, several combining arginine with Pycnogenol or yohimbine ([Rhim 2019](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/30770070)). The American Urological Association is cooler: its 2018 guideline, still the current one, lists ginseng, yohimbine and "l-arginine alone or in combination with other substances" among treatments that "either are ineffective, are not safe, or lack a sufficient body of evidence" and warns their use "may preclude the use of other treatments known to be effective" ([AUA 2018 ED Guideline](https://www.auanet.org/documents/guidelines/ed%20website%20final.pdf)).

L-citrulline is the pharmacologically smarter way to deliver arginine but has almost no ED evidence of its own. Oral citrulline raises plasma arginine more effectively than oral arginine because it is not destroyed by intestinal and hepatic arginase; 3 g twice daily raised the arginine/ADMA ratio from 186 to 278 and increased urinary nitrate and cGMP in healthy volunteers ([Schwedhelm 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2291275/)), and single doses up to 15 g caused no side effects ([Moinard 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17953788/)). The entire human ED literature, however, is one single-blind study of 24 men with mild ED in which **1.5 g/day for one month improved erection hardness from grade 3 to 4 in 50% versus 8.3% on placebo**, with the authors themselves calling citrulline "less effective than phosphodiesterase type-5 enzyme inhibitors" ([Cormio 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21195829/)). Examine.com grades citrulline for ED on that single study ([Examine citrulline](https://examine.com/supplements/citrulline/)), the 2024 network meta-analysis did not identify it as effective ([Barbonetti 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39279185/)), and the 3–6 g/day doses consumer sites recommend are extrapolated from blood-pressure and exercise studies rather than ED trials ([Cleveland Clinic citrulline](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/citrulline-benefits)).

Korean red ginseng (Panax ginseng) is the only herb with Cochrane-level evidence, and that evidence calls the effect trivial. Across 9 RCTs and 587 men with mild-to-moderate ED treated for up to 12 weeks, ginseng improved IIEF-EF by **3.52 points** (95% CI 1.79–5.25) against an assumed MCID of 4 and IIEF-5 by 2.39 against an MCID of 5, though self-reported ability to have intercourse rose two-and-a-half-fold (RR 2.55), leading Cochrane to conclude ginseng "may only have trivial effects on erectile function or satisfaction with intercourse" with no trial comparing it to a PDE5 inhibitor ([Lee et al., Cochrane 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33871063/)). The classic trial used **900 mg three times daily** for 8 weeks and found 60% of men reporting improved erections alongside RigiScan rigidity gains ([Hong 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12394711/)). Mayo Clinic rates Panax ginseng "studied in people, positive results, generally safe" for up to 6 months, with insomnia, headache and vertigo as common side effects ([Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/erectile-dysfunction/in-depth/erectile-dysfunction-herbs/art-20044394)), while Examine's blunt verdict is that ginseng is "subpar for erectile function" ([Examine Panax ginseng](https://examine.com/supplements/panax-ginseng/)).

The Pycnogenol-plus-arginine combination sold as Prelox has the largest reported effect and the weakest independence. A 2023 meta-analysis of three RCTs (184 men) found a pooled **+8.9 IIEF-EF points** over placebo (95% CI 4.14–13.66), a figure as large as on-demand PDE5 inhibitors; the two trials driving it (a 50-man one-month crossover and a 124-man six-month study, both at **80 mg Pycnogenol plus 2.8–3 g L-arginine aspartate daily**) come from the Horphag-linked research network, one was rated high risk of bias, and Egger's test suggested small-study effects (P = 0.033) ([Frontiers in Endocrinology 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10614297/); [Ledda 2010](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/20184576)). The Japanese trial at a quarter of that arginine dose (690 mg with 60 mg Pycnogenol) was not significantly better than placebo on the erectile domain ([Aoki 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21618639/)), and the manufacturer concedes the benefit "largely faded" within a month of stopping ([Prelox.com](https://www.prelox.com/mobile/research)). No RCT tests Pycnogenol alone for ED. A Viagra-sized effect from a nutrient whose arginine component yields 3–4 points on its own should be treated as hypothesis-generating until an independent group replicates it.

The rest of the herbal market is thinner still. Tribulus terrestris pooled to **+3.23 IIEF-5** across 8 trials with no testosterone effect ([Suharyani 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40360723/)), but the estimate hinges on a 180-man phase IV trial of the manufacturer's own Tribestan at **1,500 mg/day** ([Kamenov 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28364864/)); the independent 30-man trial at 800 mg/day was negative ([Santos 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24630840/)). Maca's only ED trial (50 men, 2.4 g/day, 12 weeks) improved IIEF-5 by **+1.6 versus +0.5** on placebo ([Zenico 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19260845/)). Saffron failed its one large test: in 346 men, 30 mg twice daily improved no IIEF domain, and 4.2% answered "yes" to the global efficacy question versus 91.2% on sildenafil ([Safarinejad 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20520621/)). Tongkat ali's pooled IIEF-5 change was non-significant (+0.91, 95% CI −1.50 to 3.33) ([Kotirum 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26365449/)). Fenugreek (Testofen 600 mg/day) and ashwagandha (KSM-66 300 mg twice daily) improve libido scores and modestly raise testosterone in men *without* ED ([Rao 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26791805/); [Khanna 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41766918/)); they are libido products, not ED treatments. Horny goat weed has no human ED trial at all, its evidence being in-vitro PDE5 inhibition by semi-synthetic icariin derivatives ([Dell'Agli 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18778098/)), and both placebo-controlled ginkgo trials were negative ([Wheatley 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15378664/)). The market reflects none of this: an analysis of 25 products sold in Italy found **80% matched a "no expected efficacy" profile**, and arginine, present in 63% of products, "was always included in a dose below the minimally effective amount" ([Petre 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37686709/); [Examine summary](https://examine.com/research-feed/study/9kKr39/)).

The fairest comparator for a 3-point supplement is not Viagra but lifestyle, and here supplements come out roughly even or behind. Aerobic exercise of 30–60 minutes, 3–5 times a week for a median six months improved IIEF-EF by **+2.8 points** over non-exercising controls, rising to **+4.9 in severe ED** ([Khera 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37814532/)). Pelvic-floor muscle training with biofeedback produced **+6.7 IIEF-EF points at three months** and restored normal function in 40% of men by six months in a 55-man RCT ([Dorey 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15527607/)) — a bigger effect than any supplement, and the EAU calls it "a promising alternative to pharmacological treatments" ([EAU Guidelines](https://uroweb.org/guidelines/sexual-and-reproductive-health/chapter/management-of-erectile-dysfunction)). Obese men who cut BMI from 36.9 to 31.2 over two years gained 3 points on controls and **31% versus 5% regained IIEF ≥22** ([Esposito 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15213209/)); statins added **+3.4 IIEF-5** across 11 RCTs ([Kostis 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24684744/)); 25% of smokers who quit improved within a year versus none who continued ([Pourmand 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15610111/)); and 13 of 35 men with metabolic syndrome on a Mediterranean diet versus 2 of 30 controls reached IIEF ≥22 at two years ([Esposito 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16395320/)). These interventions treat the underlying vascular disease and stack with any supplement or drug.

## Certified single-ingredient products beat every enhancement blend on dose, testing and price

Once the evidence is reduced to "arginine or citrulline at 2.5–6 g, ginseng at 1–3 g, maybe Pycnogenol with arginine," the product question becomes simple: which bottles deliver those doses with verifiable testing? The answer is a short list of commodity single ingredients, and almost none of the products marketed *as* ED pills make it. Among nitric-oxide precursors, Informed Sport's certified-product list carries **KAGED Citrulline**, **PODIUM L-Citrulline** and **NOW Sports Arginine & Citrulline** ([Informed Sport certified list](https://sport.wetestyoutrust.com/node/144); [Informed Sport Nov-2026 PDF](https://sport.wetestyoutrust.com/sites/default/files/generated-pdf/file.pdf)). Kaged Fermented L-Citrulline supplies 2 g per scoop in a 200 g tub for **$15 one-time or $12 on subscription**, which works out to about **$7/month at 3 g/day** ([Innerbody citrulline guide](https://www.innerbody.com/best-l-citrulline-supplement)). NOW Sports L-Citrulline 1,200 mg tablets (120 for $24.49 on iHerb) are described by Healthline as Informed Sport Certified, though Informed Sport's own PDF names the Arginine & Citrulline combination rather than the plain tablet, so that specific listing is not independently confirmed ([iHerb](https://www.iherb.com/pr/now-foods-sports-l-citrulline-1-200-mg-120-tablets/59155); [Healthline](https://www.healthline.com/nutrition/viagra-foods)). Given the pharmacokinetic case for citrulline and its far better certification footprint, a certified citrulline powder is the pragmatic way to run an "arginine" trial, which is why Kaged tops this report's list despite citrulline's thinner clinical file.

## Two in three gas-station "herbal" pills contain sildenafil, and yohimbe is the legal ingredient that kills

The dominant safety hazard in this market is not a botanical at all; it is undeclared prescription drugs. FDA's Sexual Enhancement and Energy Product Notifications database listed **441 products** on 7 November 2026 — about 59% of all 745 categorized hidden-ingredient notifications — and FDA states these products "are likely to be contaminated with dangerous hidden ingredients" and that the list "covers only a small fraction of the contaminated products on the market" ([FDA notifications](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/medication-health-fraud-notifications/sexual-enhancement-and-energy-product-notifications)). An analysis of FDA warnings from 2007–2016 found 353 of 776 adulterated supplements were sexual-enhancement products, 97.6% did not declare the drug on the label, 81% of the sexual products contained sildenafil or an analogue, and 28 products were caught two or three separate times ([Tucker et al., JAMA Network Open 2018](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2706496)). Independent purchases tell the same story: of 91 "herbal/natural" sexual-performance products, **81% contained PDE5-inhibitor pharmaceuticals**, 18 of them above 110% of the highest approved drug strength ([Campbell 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23634714/)). A California public-health survey of 102 products bought around Sacramento found **67% adulterated**, with the risk concentrated by channel and format: **88% of products from gas stations, liquor stores and mini-marts versus 7% from pharmacies and health stores, and 89% of single-pill blister packs versus 10% of bottled products**; 46% of positives contained both sildenafil and tadalafil, 42% carried doses at or above the minimum prescription dose, 72% of products already on FDA's list were still adulterated, and "made in USA" claims and label warnings offered no protection ([Fischer et al. 2023](https://www.dovepress.com/cross-sectional-survey-of-adulterated-sexual-enhancement-products-sold-peer-reviewed-fulltext-article-NDS)). The 2026 notices continue the pattern — 10 Day Forecast (sildenafil plus acetaminophen), Candy Power For Man (tadalafil), MAXMAN Coffee (sildenafil plus tadalafil), Anaconda Premium 500K (tadalafil), most sold on eBay ([FDA 10 Day Forecast](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/medication-health-fraud-notifications/10-day-forecast-may-be-harmful-due-hidden-ingredients); [FDA MAXMAN Coffee](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/medication-health-fraud-notifications/maxman-coffee-may-be-harmful-due-hidden-drug-ingredients)) — and FDA's December 2023 warning letter to Amazon confirmed tadalafil or sildenafil in seven products shipped through Fulfillment by Amazon ([FDA Warning Letter to Amazon](https://www.fda.gov/inspections-compliance-enforcement-and-criminal-investigations/warning-letters/amazoncom-inc-662503-12202023)). "Honey packs" have become the signature format, with roughly 20 FDA notices covering products like Royal Honey, Black Thai Honey and Secret Miracle Honey, several combining sildenafil, tadalafil and acetaminophen ([FDA tainted honey products](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/health-fraud-scams/tainted-honey-based-products-hidden-active-drug-ingredients)). Even the published "herbal" literature is contaminated: a Butea superba trial that initially matched sildenafil's response rate lost its effect with a second batch, and the supplier confirmed the first batch had been blended with a PDE5 inhibitor ([Cortés-González 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19624593/)).

Hidden drugs hurt people precisely because they reach the men a doctor would never prescribe them to. Sildenafil and tadalafil are contraindicated with nitrates "in any form" and potentiate alpha-blockers and antihypertensives ([Viagra prescribing information](https://labeling.pfizer.com/ShowLabeling.aspx?format=PDF&id=652)); FDA's standard notice warns the combination "may lower blood pressure to dangerous levels," which "is especially concerning for people with diabetes, high blood pressure, high cholesterol or heart disease, who commonly take nitrate-containing medications" ([FDA MAXMAN Coffee](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/medication-health-fraud-notifications/maxman-coffee-may-be-harmful-due-hidden-drug-ingredients)). Rhino-branded pills have carried sildenafil or tadalafil at **14 to 200 times** the labeled prescription limit, and a 40-year-old man with no nitrate use suffered an inferior STEMI with complete heart block after three Rhino pills ([Deliwala et al., Cureus 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7212751/)); FDA has received reports of chest pain, severe headaches and prolonged erections requiring surgery after Rhino products ([FDA Rhino warning](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/fda-warns-consumers-to-avoid-rhino-male-enhancement-products-found-at-retailers-because-of-undeclared-and-potentially-dangerous-drug-ingredients-300755962.html)). Nationally, dietary supplements cause an estimated **23,005 emergency-department visits a year**; sexual-enhancement or bodybuilding products account for 14.1% of such visits among men, and 37.3% of the sexual-enhancement visits involved cardiac symptoms ([Geller et al., NEJM 2015](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMsa1504267)). More than 80 unapproved sildenafil analogues — hydroxythiohomosildenafil, thiosildenafil, sulfoaildenafil and others — appear in these products, and their "efficacy and toxic effects have not been clinically assessed" ([Fischer et al. 2023](https://www.dovepress.com/cross-sectional-survey-of-adulterated-sexual-enhancement-products-sold-peer-reviewed-fulltext-article-NDS); [Drug Testing and Analysis 2020](https://analyticalsciencejournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/dta.2806)).

Among legal ingredients, yohimbe is the outlier that should be treated like a hidden drug. California poison centers logged 238 symptomatic yohimbine cases in 2000–2006, 98.7% from herbal products; severe outcomes were **5.8 times** more likely than for average exposures, with tachycardia in 43%, hypertension in 25% and anxiety or agitation in 33% ([Kearney 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20442348/)). Of 49 retail yohimbine brands, only 22% stated a quantity and **4.1% stated it accurately**, with actual content ranging from undetectable to 12.1 mg per serving ([Cohen 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26391406/)). NCCIH links yohimbine to arrhythmia, blood-pressure problems, heart attacks and seizures and notes it is illegal to market an over-the-counter yohimbine product for ED in the US ([NCCIH Yohimbe](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/yohimbe)); the EU placed yohimbe bark in Annex III of its fortified-foods regulation after EFSA could not set a safe intake ([Commission Regulation (EU) 2015/403](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2015/403)); the AUA states flatly that "Yohimbine is not recommended" ([AUA Management of ED update](https://www.auanet.org/documents/education/clinical-guidance/erectile-dysfunction.pdf)); and poison-control specialists report "Cardiac collapse with sudden death... in multiple case reports" with no antidote ([Tennessee Poison Center](https://www.vumc.org/poison-control/toxicology-question-week/feb-15-2022-yohimbine-or-no-himbine-potentially-fatal-over-counter)). Any product listing yohimbe or yohimbine — ExtenZe included — fails the safety requirement outright.

The recommended ingredients carry real but manageable risks, and three of them define who should not self-treat. L-arginine's one mortality-level signal is the VINTAGE MI trial, in which **6 of 70 heart-attack survivors (8.6%) on 9 g/day died within six months versus none on placebo**, prompting early termination and the conclusion that "L-arginine should not be recommended following acute myocardial infarction" ([VINTAGE MI, JAMA 2006](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/202136)); Mayo Clinic accordingly says arginine "isn't recommended for people who have had a recent heart attack," warns of additive hypotension with nitrates, blood-pressure drugs and sildenafil ("Don't take L-arginine with sildenafil"), hyperkalaemia with potassium-sparing diuretics, and bleeding with anticoagulants ([Mayo Clinic L-arginine](https://www.mayoclinic.org/drugs-supplements-l-arginine/art-20364681); [Mayo Clinic ED supplements](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/erectile-dysfunction/in-depth/erectile-dysfunction-herbs/art-20044394)), and Harvard Health concludes "Men — especially those at risk for heart disease — should avoid these supplements" ([Harvard Health](https://www.health.harvard.edu/mens-health/beware-of-erectile-dysfunction-scams)). L-citrulline's interaction with PDE5 inhibitors and nitrates is rated "Major — Do not take this combination" ([RxList L-citrulline](https://www.rxlist.com/supplements/l-citrulline.htm)). In fairness, the trials that deliberately combined 2.5–5 g arginine with tadalafil or sildenafil recorded gastritis and insomnia, not hypotensive events ([Gallo 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7261690/); [El-Wakeel 2020](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/31267684)), so the clinical magnitude at supplement doses appears small but is unmeasured; everyday side effects are GI upset above single doses of about 9 g, which divided dosing eliminates ([Grimble 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17513449/)), and NIH notes "The safety of taking high-dose arginine supplements for more than 3 months is not known" ([NIH ODS](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/ExerciseAndAthleticPerformance-HealthProfessional/)). Ginseng's documented interaction is with warfarin — American ginseng lowered INR in a randomized trial of healthy volunteers ([Yuan 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15238367/)) — and NCCIH adds that it may lower blood sugar, worsen autoimmune disease and interfere with clotting, with insomnia the commonest complaint ([NCCIH Asian ginseng](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/asian-ginseng)). Pycnogenol is well tolerated across the Prelox trials with no adverse events in 184 men, with only theoretical bleeding and glucose-lowering cautions ([Frontiers 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10614297/); [Examine Pycnogenol](https://examine.com/supplements/pycnogenol/)).

The "testosterone" herbs carry the liver signals. Ashwagandha is "implicated in cases of clinically apparent liver injury" appearing 2–12 weeks after starting, usually cholestatic and self-limited but with fatal acute liver failure described ([LiverTox: Ashwagandha](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK548536/)); Denmark banned it from supplements on thyroid and sex-hormone grounds, and Sweden followed ([NutraIngredients 2023](https://nutraingredients.com/Article/2023/05/17/Growing-concerns-over-safety-of-ashwagandha-in-EU-member-states)); NCCIH advises men with hormone-sensitive prostate cancer to avoid it ([NCCIH Ashwagandha](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/ashwagandha)). Tongkat ali has a published case of jaundice resolving on discontinuation ([Kaliounji 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38646387/)), an EFSA opinion flagging DNA-damage potential in animals and liver and kidney effects in chronic toxicity testing ([EFSA 2021](https://efsa.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.2903/j.efsa.2021.6937)), and reports of lead, mercury and undeclared sildenafil analogues in Malaysian products ([Examine Tongkat ali](https://examine.com/supplements/tongkat-ali/)). Tribulus has case reports of severe liver and kidney injury — a 46-year-old man reached a bilirubin of 48 mg/dL and creatinine of 7.1 after two months of daily use ([Mohy-Ud-Din 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38328764/)) — although LiverTox judges the link unconvincing at typical doses ([LiverTox: Tribulus](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK583201/)). Fenugreek is the cleanest of the group (LiverTox likelihood E, "unlikely cause") but can drop blood sugar and trigger allergy ([LiverTox: Fenugreek](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK548826/); [NCCIH Fenugreek](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/fenugreek)). DHEA, a common blend ingredient, earns a Mayo Clinic "Avoid" because high or long-term use "might raise the risk of hormone-sensitive cancers, such as prostate and breast cancers" ([Mayo Clinic DHEA](https://www.mayoclinic.org/drugs-supplements-dhea/art-20364199)), and zinc, often dosed at 24–45 mg in blends, has a tolerable upper intake of 40 mg/day from all sources before copper deficiency becomes a risk ([NIH ODS Zinc](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-Consumer/)). Liver injury has also been attributed to the blends themselves — drug-induced autoimmune hepatitis with ExtenZe and liver injury in a patient self-medicating with Nugenix ([Pharmacy Times](https://www.pharmacytimes.com/view/association-found-between-otc-male-enhancement-supplementation-and-liver-injuries)). No supplement ED trial of any ingredient has run longer than six months, so the safety of years of daily use is extrapolated rather than demonstrated.

## Only BSCG and Informed Sport screen every lot for hidden PDE5 inhibitors

Not all third-party seals answer the question a man buying an ED supplement actually has, which is "does this bottle contain Viagra?" The USP Verified Mark confirms a product "contains the ingredients listed on the label in the declared strength and amounts," is free of harmful levels of heavy metals and pesticides, was made under FDA GMPs and dissolves properly — nothing about drug screening ([USP Verified Mark](https://www.usp.org/verification-services/verified-mark)). NSF/ANSI 173 "Contents Certified," ConsumerLab's seal and Labdoor's Purity/Accuracy certifications likewise verify identity, potency and contaminants; a comparison site notes "ConsumerLab does not screen for banned substances specifically" ([NSF FAQs](https://www.nsf.org/about-nsf/faqs/dietary-supplements-functional-foods-faqs); [ConsumerLab About](https://www.consumerlab.com/about/); [Nutrola comparison](https://nutrola.app/en/blog/usp-nsf-informed-sport-consumerlab-certifications-compared-2026)). NIH summarizes the limit: these seals "do not guarantee that a product is safe or effective" ([NIH ODS: What You Need to Know](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/WYNTK-Consumer/)). The programs that do screen finished products for drugs are the sport "banned substance" certifications. **NSF Certified for Sport** tests for 290+ banned substances, requires label-claim and contaminant verification and annual GMP audits, and is the only program recognized by USADA, MLB and the NHL, but its lot-testing frequency is "variable by product and manufacturer" ([NSF Certified for Sport](https://www.nsfsport.com/our-mark.php); [BSCG comparison](https://www.bscg.org/blogs/single/independent-supplement-testing-what-it-is-and-who-does-it)). **Informed Sport** tests "every single batch of a product prior to it being released to market" for 285+ substances in ISO 17025 labs, whereas the related Informed Choice mark is a retail blind-monitoring scheme in which "it is not guaranteed that every batch has been tested" ([Informed Sport vs Informed Choice](https://sport.wetestyoutrust.com/about/sport-vs-choice)). **BSCG Certified Drug Free** is the most explicit: it tests every lot for 450+ substances, and its published menu names **tadalafil, vardenafil, avanafil, udenafil, dapoxetine, sibutramine, phenolphthalein and dozens of sildenafil analogues** including hydroxythiohomosildenafil and thiosildenafil, stating that "All drugs included in Amazon dietary supplement compliance API testing requirements are covered" ([BSCG testing menu](https://www.bscg.org/bscg-banned-substance-testing-menu-v070725)). Amazon itself now requires third-party PDE5-inhibitor screening for sexual-enhancement listings ([BSCG](https://www.bscg.org/blogs/single/independent-supplement-testing-what-it-is-and-who-does-it); [Vivariant](https://vivariant.com/home/supplement-pde5-inhibitor-screening/)), which is one reason the certified products in this report are commodity amino acids and botanicals rather than anything sold as a "male enhancement" pill.

A logo on the bottle proves little; the lot number does. BSCG states that "possession of a certification mark on packaging alone is insufficient evidence of compliance" and that certification "applies only to tested products and defined lots," while claims like "WADA approved" or "guaranteed drug free" overstate what any program does ([BSCG comparison](https://www.bscg.org/blogs/single/independent-supplement-testing-what-it-is-and-who-does-it)); Informed Sport adds that "A label stating 'Safe for Sports People', or 'Approved by WADA' is meaningless" ([Informed Sport](https://sport.wetestyoutrust.com/)). USADA's advice to athletes applies directly to consumers: scan the label with the NSF Certified for Sport app "to make sure it's a certified product and lot number" ([USADA Supplement Connect](https://www.usada.org/substances/supplement-connect/reduce-risk-testing-positive-experiencing-adverse-health-effects/)); Informed Sport batches are searchable at sport.wetestyoutrust.com, BSCG keeps a public lot database, and USP products appear in its Verified Product Finder. Healthline's dietitians give the practical rule: "if a company's sole assertion of third-party testing is a claim on its website that doesn't link to a specific certification program, you may want to choose another product" ([Healthline best L-arginine](https://www.healthline.com/nutrition/best-l-arginine)) — which is why Nutricost's, Nootropics Depot's and Healthy Origins' "third-party tested" statements rank below Kaged's searchable Informed Sport listing, and why Double Wood's published per-lot ISO 17025 certificate of analysis is the strongest of the self-reported approaches found ([Double Wood](https://doublewoodsupplements.com/products/lj100-tongkat-ali-extract)). FDA adds a caution of its own: "Be cautious when a product is marketed as being manufactured in an FDA-approved facility; FDA does not approve facilities" ([FDA: Avoiding contaminated products](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/medication-health-fraud/avoiding-products-contaminated-hidden-ingredients)).

Label scrutiny comes next. A proprietary blend that hides amounts is disqualifying in this category: it is how VigRX Plus, ExtenZe and Life Extension's Prelox avoid showing whether they meet trial doses, and hidden quantities were the norm among the most dangerous legal ingredient (only 22% of yohimbine products listed any amount) ([Cohen 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26391406/)). The label should state a per-serving amount that can be multiplied to a studied regimen — 2.5–6 g arginine, 1.5–3 g citrulline, 1–3 g standardized ginseng extract with the ginsenoside percentage declared, 80 mg Pycnogenol with about 2.8 g arginine aspartate — and the buyer should remember that citrulline malate is only 56.64% citrulline by weight and that malate products have averaged a 1.6:1 ratio despite 2:1 claims ([NIH ODS](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/ExerciseAndAthleticPerformance-HealthProfessional/); [Innerbody citrulline guide](https://www.innerbody.com/best-l-citrulline-supplement)). Any mention of yohimbe, yohimbine or DHEA ends the evaluation. A survey of top-selling online ED supplements found 8 of 21 ingredients (38%) had no published studies at all ([Balasubramanian 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9555224/)), so a long ingredient list is a negative, not a positive, signal.

The red flags are empirical, not folk wisdom. Single-dose blister packs sold near the register of a gas station, liquor store or corner market were adulterated 88–89% of the time against 7–10% for bottled products from pharmacies and health stores ([Fischer et al. 2023](https://www.dovepress.com/cross-sectional-survey-of-adulterated-sexual-enhancement-products-sold-peer-reviewed-fulltext-article-NDS)). Names that evoke drugs or potency — "Herb Viagra" (contained sildenafil), more than 25 "Rhino" variants since 2007, numbers like "5000," "500K" or "700000," and the words honey, coffee, candy or shot — recur across FDA notices from 2018 to 2026 ([Chiang 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5313300/); [FDA Rhino warning](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/fda-warns-consumers-to-avoid-rhino-male-enhancement-products-found-at-retailers-because-of-undeclared-and-potentially-dangerous-drug-ingredients-300755962.html)). FDA tells consumers to be wary of products "that offer immediate or quick results," that carry "multiple rave reviews" or spread through social media, and that lean on "herbal" or "all natural" ([FDA: Avoiding contaminated products](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/medication-health-fraud/avoiding-products-contaminated-hidden-ingredients)). The pharmacology supplies one more heuristic: no botanical in this report produced a measurable effect in less than four weeks, so a product that "works in 30 minutes" is behaving like sildenafil because it probably contains it. The sequence a careful buyer should follow is therefore: search FDA's Health Fraud Product Database for the product name, confirm the exact product and lot in the certifier's database (BSCG, Informed Sport or NSF Certified for Sport for drug screening; USP, NSF Contents Certified or ConsumerLab for identity and contaminants), buy a bottled single ingredient from a pharmacy, health store or the brand's own site rather than a third-party marketplace seller, reject proprietary blends and yohimbe, and report any chest pain, fainting, severe headache or prolonged erection to emergency services and FDA MedWatch ([FDA Health Fraud Product Database](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/health-fraud-scams/health-fraud-product-database); [FDA Anaconda Premium 500K](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/medication-health-fraud-notifications/anaconda-premium-500k-may-be-harmful-due-hidden-ingredient)).

The strongest argument against self-treating with anything, supplement or drug, is what ED signals. In the placebo arm of the Prostate Cancer Prevention Trial (9,457 men over 55), incident ED carried an adjusted hazard ratio of 1.25 and incident-or-prevalent ED 1.45 for cardiovascular events — "in the range of risk associated with current smoking or a family history of myocardial infarction" ([Thompson 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16414947/)). A meta-analysis of 14 studies and 92,757 men found relative risks of **1.44 for total cardiovascular events, 1.62 for myocardial infarction, 1.39 for stroke and 1.25 for all-cause mortality**, higher in younger men ([Vlachopoulos 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23300267/)). The lead time is long enough to act on: among men with angiographic coronary disease, ED appeared before cardiac symptoms in 67%, by a mean of **38.8 months** ([Montorsi 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12932937/)), and in the COBRA study by 24 months in 93% of chronic coronary patients ([Montorsi 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16854949/)). Princeton IV (2024) therefore instructs clinicians "to treat men with ED as being at risk for cardiac events until proven otherwise," with ASCVD risk scoring for every man with vasculogenic ED and coronary artery calcium scoring for those at borderline-to-intermediate risk ([Köhler et al., Princeton IV](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39115509/); [ACC Expert Analysis](https://www.acc.org/latest-in-cardiology/articles/2024/09/23/10/45/erectile-disfunction-as-an-ascvd-risk-enhancing-factor)), and the AUA's Statement 3 makes counselling about ED as a cardiovascular marker a clinical principle ([AUA 2018 ED Guideline](https://www.auanet.org/documents/guidelines/ed%20website%20final.pdf)). A supplement ordered online spends that two-to-five-year window doing nothing about the arteries.

The work-up also catches causes no supplement can touch. The AUA requires a morning total testosterone in all men with ED, with deficiency defined as **<300 ng/dL on at least two morning samples** plus symptoms, and notes PDE5 inhibitors work better when deficiency is corrected ([AUA 2018 ED Guideline](https://www.auanet.org/documents/guidelines/ed%20website%20final.pdf)); the EAU adds fasting glucose or HbA1c and a lipid profile if not done in the past year, with PSA, prolactin and LH in selected men ([EAU Guidelines](https://uroweb.org/guidelines/sexual-and-reproductive-health/chapter/management-of-erectile-dysfunction)). A medication review often finds the culprit: thiazides are "the most common cause of erectile dysfunction among the high blood pressure medicines," followed by beta-blockers ([MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/004024.htm)), and sexual dysfunction affects **80.3% of men on sertraline** and 78.6% on citalopram versus 37% on escitalopram ([J Fam Pract summary of Serretti & Chiesa](https://cdn.mdedge.com/files/s3fs-public/Document/September-2017/6211_JFP_ClinInquiry1.pdf)) — problems solved by switching drugs, not by adding ginseng. Peyronie's disease is present in 7.9% of men presenting with ED and 20.3% of diabetic men with ED ([AUA Peyronie's Guideline](https://www.auanet.org/guidelines-and-quality/guidelines/peyronies-disease-guideline)), lower urinary tract symptoms accompany up to 72% of ED ([AUA ED Guideline](https://www.auanet.org/guidelines-and-quality/guidelines/erectile-dysfunction-(ed)-guideline)), and sleep apnoea, in which 59–69% of men have ED, responds to CPAP ([Int J Impot Res 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41443-026-01315-7)). The men who most need the visit are those with new ED under about 50 (Mayo's urologists say they "should be screened for heart disease before starting any treatment"), anyone on nitrates or recently post-MI (for whom both hidden PDE5 inhibitors and L-arginine are hazardous), men with chest pain or breathlessness on exertion, sudden-onset ED, low libido with fatigue suggesting hypogonadism, penile curvature or pain, and anyone whose 12-week supplement trial has produced nothing ([Mayo Clinic Q&A](https://newsnetwork.mayoclinic.org/discussion/mayo-clinic-q-and-a-some-products-that-claim-to-treat-erectile-dysfunction-can-be-harmful/)). NCCIH's bottom line applies to the whole category: "No complementary health approaches have been shown to be safe and effective for sexual enhancement or treating ED. Safety is a serious concern" ([NCCIH](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/erectile-dysfunctionsexual-enhancement)).

## Conclusion

The market has the evidence inverted. The ingredients with the only guideline recognition and the only statistically significant pooled effects — L-arginine, its citrulline precursor and standardized Korean red ginseng — are commodity powders costing $6–12 a month, while the $50–70 "male enhancement" blends that dominate advertising systematically under-dose those same ingredients, hide the amounts, or substitute herbs with no human trials, and pay for the reviews that rank them. The premium buys marketing, not erections. The "natural alternative" framing is backwards too: the category's dominant hazard is undeclared sildenafil at uncontrolled doses sold to exactly the men whose heart disease, diabetes or nitrate prescriptions make it most dangerous, which means the safest possible supplement is one so boring — a single amino acid from a sports-certified brand — that nobody would bother spiking it.

The right mental model for a man who still prefers a supplement is a 90-day experiment with a known floor and a known ceiling. The floor is a cardiovascular and hormonal baseline from a clinician, which the two-to-five-year lead time between ED and coronary events makes non-negotiable rather than optional; the ceiling is a 3–4 point IIEF gain that only reliably appears in mild ED, that roughly matches what six months of aerobic exercise delivers and that pelvic-floor training with biofeedback exceeds, so the supplement should be the smallest part of the plan, not the plan. If 12 weeks of certified citrulline or arginine plus exercise and pelvic-floor work have not moved things, the honest next step is not a different bottle but the $6-a-month generic tadalafil that outperforms every supplement on efficacy, tolerability data and price — a reversal of the usual assumption that the prescription is the expensive, risky option. Two things could change this picture: the registered citrulline-versus-tadalafil trial (NCT07642505) would give citrulline its first real test, and the AUA's "Disorders of Penile Erection" guideline due in 2027 will show whether a decade of small positive arginine trials has moved American urology off its 2018 position that these agents remain unproven.
